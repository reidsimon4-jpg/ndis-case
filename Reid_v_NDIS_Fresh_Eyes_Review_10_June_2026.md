## 5. LEGAL RESEARCH — all verified 10 June 2026 (URLs in §9)

5.1 **The s 70 fight: the law is now pinned.** ART Act 2024 (Cth) **s 70** is the non-disclosure power; **s 70(3)** expressly permits orders prohibiting disclosure "to some or all of the parties" — watch for the Respondent seeking a party-facing order. **s 71** lists mandatory considerations, including "the principle that it is desirable that evidence given before the Tribunal and the contents of documents given to the Tribunal are made available to **all the parties**" (s 71(2)(c)). Oppose any party-facing restriction on s 71(2)(a)–(c), the four waiver grounds already filed, and *Adachi Disability Services v Commissioner NDIS QSC* [2024] FCA 311 (suppression refused in an NDIS banning context; open justice prevailed). Summons/production = **s 74** (14 days' notice; Tribunal may act at a party's request); inspection of produced documents = s 78; referral of a question of law to the FCA = **s 185** (requires President's agreement); dismissal power = s 100.

5.2 **The single most important new authority: *Abdullah and Commissioner, NDIS QSC* [2026] ARTA 743 (6 May 2026)** — the ART's most recent substantive s 73ZN decision (verified on AustLII, quotes checked against the full text):
- The Tribunal treated procedural-fairness complaints about the Commission's process as "**'cured' by the Tribunal standing in the shoes** of the decision-maker".
- The Act "is **fundamentally protective in character**", and the Commissioner must act in a "**risk responsive and proportionate**" manner — arguably extending to the Tribunal on review.
- Duration was assessed by analogy to *ASIC v Adler* [2002] NSWSC 483 disqualification banding.
**Consequence for our framing:** a stand-alone "the delegate acted for an improper purpose" complaint risks the *Abdullah* "cured" answer. In the ART, the Halls/Tear material must be deployed as: (1) an attack on the **reliability, completeness and weight** of the Commission's evidence — serious allegations need cogent proof (*Briginshaw v Briginshaw* (1938) 60 CLR 336); (2) proof there is **no present protective need** — under *Frugtniet v ASIC* [2019] HCA 16 and *Shi v MARA* [2008] HCA 31 the Tribunal must itself, today, be satisfied the protective purpose is engaged, and tainted/competitor-instigated evidence cannot discharge that; (3) duration/proportionality context. The pure improper-purpose doctrine (Padfield; R v Toohey; *Thompson v Randwick Corporation* (1950) 81 CLR 87 — note the correct party name is "Corporation", and the "substantial purpose" test at [11]) stays in reserve for judicial review/misfeasance forums. This refines, but does not contradict, the 29 May sequenced strategy.

5.3 **Mootness/utility (FFG, and later the individuals).** The ART Act has **no mootness provision**; *Shi* confirms the Tribunal decides on circumstances as they exist at decision time; and r 9(2)(b) of the NDIS (Provider Registration and Practice Standards) Rules 2018 makes "whether a banning order has **ever** been in force" a mandatory consideration in any future registration application (quoted in *Sunshine Disability* [2025] ARTA 2475). The AGS's own 13 Feb 2026 letter supplies the continuing-effect evidence: expired orders stay on the register "**indefinitely**", removal only "in exceptional cases" and "solely by the NDIS Commission". Quote it back. Note also the slow-motion mootness clock for the individuals: Simon's and Vida's orders expire 21/22 Dec 2027 — interlocutory drift is itself a Commission win; press for a 2026 hearing date.

5.4 **The s 73ZN landscape is thin — and that is an opportunity.** Only ~10 published decisions mention s 73ZN; only two substantive merits decisions exist (*Abdullah* [2026] ARTA 743; *Johnston* [2024] AATA 3438), both affirmations against under-resourced applicants; **no published decision has ever set aside a s 73ZN banning order**. Stays: *Jonah* [2025] ARTA 2582 (ART Act s 32(2); refused — public interest in enforcement outweighed individual circumstances); but *Adachi* [2024] FCA 311 shows the Federal Court will briefly suspend a banning order where a serious case is shown. **Correction:** the phrase "broad protective purpose" is not a case-law phrase (it comes from the Commission's own Banning Order Policy) — the judicial formulation is *Abdullah*'s "fundamentally protective in character". Do not cite "broad protective purpose" as authority.
