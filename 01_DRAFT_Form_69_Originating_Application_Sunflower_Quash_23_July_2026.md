## Concise grounds

1. Section 73ZN(2), as it stood at the relevant dates, empowered the Respondent to prohibit or restrict a person from engaging in specified activities when a statutory condition was satisfied.

2. Each varied order instead prohibits the relevant Applicant from having “any involvement with or in the NDIS sector, in any capacity”, and extends expressly to indirect conduct, assistance “of any form”, and an inclusive rather than exhaustive list.

3. The varied orders do not identify the whole of the activities prohibited with the certainty required of coercive instruments carrying serious consequences, and impermissibly prohibit relationships or indirect involvement rather than engagement by the named person in specified activities.

4. The conclusions in grounds 2 and 3 follow from *Sunflower Care Services Pty Ltd v Commissioner of the NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission (No 2)* [2025] FCA 1442 at [127]–[141].

5. Each review decision also misconstrued or exceeded the power, and was legally unreasonable, because it failed to establish an evident and intelligible, personal and activity-specific connection between:

    a. specified activities posing risk;

    b. attributes or conduct of the particular Applicant creating that risk if the Applicant engaged in those activities;

    c. why the risk was unacceptable and could not adequately be mitigated by narrower action, conditions or restrictions; and

    d. the breadth, conditions and duration of the order made.

6. The Second Applicant’s original order is separately invalid because “directly or indirectly” is not the statutory nexus in s 73ZN(2).

7. The Second Applicant was denied procedural fairness on review because she was not given a meaningful opportunity to address the decisive change from s 73ZN(2)(a)(i) to s 73ZN(2)(a)(iii), after which the reviewer treated substantial submissions directed to the former basis as unnecessary. The denial was material for the reasons in the Second Applicant’s affidavit.

8. Alternatively, the Second Applicant’s reviewer treated a stated “usual practice” as presumptively governing the selection of s 73ZN(2)(a)(iii), and failed independently to exercise the statutory judgment on her individual facts.

9. In the First Applicant’s case, the investigation record admitted limited information about his actual operational involvement. The original and review decisions nevertheless treated corporate office, bank-signatory status, likely benefit and attributed corporate liability as sufficient substitutes for personal findings and an activity-specific statutory bridge.

10. The First Applicant’s varied order contains no exception permitting him to seek, arrange, attend to or communicate about NDIS supports for his wife, Vida Reid, or their son, Alex Reid, each of whom is an NDIS participant. The same reviewer included an express exception in the Second Applicant’s order allowing her to seek supports for herself and Alex. The unexplained difference illustrates the uncertainty and disproportionate reach of “any involvement ... in any capacity”.

11. The First Applicant’s review record included a 9 February 2023 email recruiting disability support workers for Vitality. The 22 March 2023 review notice and the final reasons used that email to formulate the specific labour-hire limb. That material identified, at its highest, a labour-recruitment or labour-supply activity. It did not provide an evident and intelligible justification for prohibiting every sector involvement, direct support, funding, guidance or assistance of any form.

11A. The original order did not prohibit labour hire or all NDIS-adjacent business. The reviewer could consider the email as fresh evidence relevant to a prospective specified activity, but could not retrospectively treat conduct permitted by the original text as a breach or use “circumvention” alone to justify the later universal prohibition: *Sunflower* at [226].

12. The five-year durations were not intelligibly connected to protective risk in each prohibited activity. The review reasons instead linked duration substantially to the expected completion of external regulatory, legal or criminal processes.

13. The ongoing ART proceedings do not determine the validity of the coercive instruments presently in force. The Applicants seek a confined legality determination, not merits review. The orders have operated for more than three years, are due to expire in December 2027, and continued delay threatens the practical utility of judicial relief.
