           OUTLINE OF THE RESPONDENTS SUBMISSIONS IN SUPPORT OF REQUEST FOR
                                CONFIDENTIALITY ORDERS

1.   On 8 August 2023, the respondent sought orders pursuant to s 35 of the Administrative
     Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, in relation to a number of documents set out in a document titled
     “Index – confidential and redacted Tribunal documents” filed on behalf of the respondent.
     For the avoidance of doubt, that index sets out 9 documents which have not been provided
     to the applicants in the T documents (numbered 1 – 9) and parts of 20 documents which
     have been provided to the applicants but redacted (the information covered by the redaction
     being the information that is sought to be covered by this application) (numbered 12 -32).
     Documents 10 and 11 in the index are duplicates of documents appearing elsewhere in the
     index.

2.   By letter dated 23 August 2023, the applicants’ representative objected to the section 35
     application and proposed orders on the basis “that the proposed orders sought are broad
     and inherently prejudicial to our clients”. In this letter, the applicants also indicated that they
     “reserve their rights to determine having the proceeding stayed after the outcome of the
     section 35 proceeding”.

3.   These submissions have been prepared in advance of the interlocutory hearing listed for
     Tuesday 12 September 2023.

4.   To avoid any confusion, the respondent has filed and served Tribunal documents in all three
     applications -Mrs Vida Reid’s Tribunal bundle, which is the lead application, consists of 1155
     pages, Mr Simon Reid’s Tribunal bundle consists of 49 pages and the Tribunal bundle for
     Forward Focused Group Pty Ltd (FFG) consists of 101 pages.

     Insurance Scheme Act 2013 (NDIS Act).

      Relevant provisions of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (AAT Act)

15.   Section 35 of the AAT Act relevantly states:
          Orders for non-publication or non-disclosure

          (4) The Tribunal may, by order, give directions prohibiting or restricting the publication
              or other disclosure, including to some or all of the parties, of information that:

              (a) relates to a proceeding; and
              (b) is any of the following:
                   (i) information that comprises evidence or information about evidence;
                   (ii) information lodged with or otherwise given to the Tribunal.

          (5) In considering whether to give direction under subsection (2), (3) or (4), the Tribunal
          is to take as the basis of its consideration the principle that it is desirable:
               (a) that hearings of proceedings before the Tribunal should be held in public; and
               (b)that evidence given before the Tribunal, and the contents of documents received
               in evidence by the Tribunal, should be made available to the public and to all the
               parties; and
               (c) that the contents of documents lodged with the Tribunal should be made
               available to all the parties.

              However (and without being required to seek the views of the parties), the Tribunal
              is to pay due regard to any reasons in favour of giving such a direction, including, for
              the purposes of subsection (3) or (4), the confidential nature (if applicable) for the
              information.

      General principles

16.   In Re Pochi and Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs 1 the Tribunal, constituted by then
      President Brennan J, his honour emphasised that giving orders under (then) s35(2) was a
      “serious step” in light of (then) s35(3), and went on to say at 55:
             Yet the powers conferred upon this Tribunal by s35(2) are not intended to lie dormant - they
             are there to be exercised, albeit sparingly. The purpose of their exercise is to secure the
             Tribunal the availability of as much relevant information as possible, without violating the
             confidentiality which a party, a witness or the public is properly entitled to preserve (though a
             proper entitlement to confidentiality is not lightly established).

17.   In Re Flood and Secretary, Department of Social Security 2, the 3-member Tribunal (Senior
      Member Dwyer presiding) unanimously refused to make a s 35 order in relation to its
      reasons for decision but upheld an application for a s35(2)(b) order from the applicant in
      respect of the transcript of proceedings. The applicant was concerned that evidence he had
      given during the hearing might prejudice his position in other legal proceedings if an order
      were not made. In reaching its decision, the Tribunal said at paragraph 17:
             We consider that the legislation requires a balancing of public and private interests.

18.   In Re Pochi and Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs, the then President Brennan J
      considered the issues surrounding the granting of a confidentiality order over information to
      exclude a party to proceedings and said (at 56-57):
            Nevertheless, if an applicant is not given a full opportunity to deal with confidential information
            adverse to his interests, the probative force of the information must be particularly cogent if that
            information is to be acted upon. There are notorious risks in failing to hear an opposing view--
            slender proofs may falsely seem irrefragable, and the scales of justice may falsely seem to be
            tipped by the weight of insubstantial factors.
            In the present case, the public interest in protecting the sources of information used to combat
            crime was paramount, and it was necessary to ensure confidentiality of the evidence which
            referred to information of that kind given to Detective Jenkins.

19.   In MRWL and Australian Securities and Investments Commission 3, Senior Member Grigg
      when considering an application for section 35 orders lodged by the applicant who was
      seeking to challenge a banning order made against him said (at 123):

            The norm is that proceedings are held in public, as reflected in section 35 of the AAT Act. It is
            important for the open administration of justice that proceedings be held in public and that
            decisions are published. However, sections 35(2) — (4) establish that there may be occasions
            when privacy and non-publication orders should be made at the discretion of the Tribunal. What
            is necessary for such a discretion to be exercised will vary from matter to matter and is entirely
            dependent on the merits and specific facts of the case. Although each matter is decided on its
            own facts, other Tribunal and Court decisions may provide guidance and examples of when it
            has been considered appropriate to exercise the section 35 discretion. A consideration of other

20.   The respondent acknowledges that as a general rule the contents of documents lodged with
      the Tribunal should be made available to the public and to all the parties. However, in the
      present case, the respondent submits that striking a balance between the public and private
      interest requires the protection of the respondent’s ability to receive confidential complaints
      about providers and to liaise effectively with the National Disability Insurance Agency (NDIA)
      to enable them to perform their statutory and regulatory functions effectively. The
      respondent seeks confidentiality orders for the following reasons:

21.   As set out above, Mrs Vida Reid has been informed that further charges will be brought
      against her. These further charges directly arise as a result of the ongoing criminal
      investigation (emphasis added) undertaken by the NDIA. Confidential index documents 1 – 9
      are all documents that have either been drafted by the NDIA, summarise conversations
      between the respondent’s staff and the NDIA and are all relevant to the ongoing criminal
      investigation. To disclose this information would undermine and could prejudice the
      investigative methodology and procedures adopted by both the NDIA and the NDIS
      Commission when conducting inquiries into allegations of criminal activity involving and
      impacting on people with disability. If this type of information were made public, or even
      disclosed to the applicants, it may compromise the respondent’s ability to be an effective
      regulator, and may provide a basis for other NDIS providers and workers to evade the
      respondent’s regulatory powers and methods to addressing non-compliance in the future,
      which would be detrimental to people with disability.

22.   Furthermore, the criminal investigation has been informed by a number of things including
      complaints which have been made on a confidential basis either to the NDIA or to the
      respondent (and sometimes both). The redactions applied to documents 12 -32 as set out in
      the confidential index primarily contain information that could lead to the identification of a
      confidential complainant. While the respondent acknowledges it is desirable for proceedings
      before the Tribunal to be heard in public, in the present case the public interest in protecting
      the confidentiality of the information should prevail. Disclosing the identity of individuals who
      have provided information to the respondent on a confidential basis is likely to seriously
      undermine the ability of the respondent to convince individuals to provide information about
      alleged misconduct in future.

23.   Finally, the respondent is aware of its ongoing disclosure obligation to the Tribunal and
      accepts that over time, the sensitivity in the documents may well change. This is particularly
      likely to be the case as the criminal prosecution against Mrs Vida Reid progresses and/or
      reaches its conclusion. With this in mind, the respondent anticipates regularly reviewing the
      documents set out in the confidential index with a view to disclosing them if and/or when, the
      grounds for this application fall away.

24.   Nevertheless, for the reasons set out above the respondent submits that the disclosure of the
      material set out in the confidential index would be contrary to the public interest.

Principles
13.   Pursuant to section 2A of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (the AAT Act),
      in carrying out its functions, the Tribunal must pursue the objective of providing a
      mechanism of review that:

       (a) is accessible; and
       (b) is fair, just, economical, informal and quick; and
       (c) is proportionate to the importance and complexity of the matter; and
       (d) promotes public trust and confidence in the decision-making of the Tribunal.

14.   Furthermore, under subsection 40(3) of the AAT Act, for the purpose of reviewing a
      decision, the Tribunal may:

        Adjourn the proceeding from time to time.

15.   The general power of a Court to control its proceedings, including the power to grant a
      temporary stay of those proceedings in various circumstances is well known. The
      Respondent therefore requests that the Tribunal exercise its discretion to stay the
      proceedings in the interests of justice where there is concurrent criminal proceedings
      on foot or where there is a “reasonable possibility” that proceedings will be instituted,
      see the cases cited in Wide Bay Conservation Council Inc v Burnett Water Pty Ltd
      (“Wide Bay”) [2010] ALMD 7901; [2008] FCA 1900 at [19]-[23].

16.   Importantly at paragraph 21, the observations of Wootten J (in McMahon v Gould
      (1982) 7 ACLR 202, a case which is cited in Wide Bay), are set out as follows:

       I approach the decision of this matter with the following guidelines:

       (a) Prima facie a plaintiff is entitled to have his action tried in the ordinary course of
           the procedure and business of the court (Rochfort v John Fairfax & Sons Ltd at
           19);

       (b) It is a grave matter to interfere with this entitlement by a stay of proceedings,
           which requires justification on proper grounds(ibid);

       (c) The burden is on the defendant in a civil action to show that it is just and
           convenient that the plaintiff’s ordinary rights should be interfered with (Jefferson v
           Bhetcha at 905);

       (d) Neither an accused (ibid) nor the Crown (Rochfort v John Fairfax & Sons Ltd at
           21) are entitled as of right to have a civil proceeding stayed because of a pending
           or possible criminal proceeding;

       (e) The court's task is one of "the balancing of justice between the parties" (Jefferson
           Ltd v Bhetcha at 904), taking account of all relevant factors (ibid at 905);


                                                                                               Page 3


                                                10
       (f) Each case must be judged on its own merits, and it would be wrong and
           undesirable to attempt to define in the abstract what are the relevant factors (ibid
           at 905);

       (g) One factor to take into account where there are pending or possible criminal
           proceedings is what is sometimes referred to as the accused's "right of silence",
           and the reasons why that right, under the law as it stands, is a right of a
           defendant in a criminal proceeding (ibid at 904). I return to this subject below;

       (h) However, the so-called "right of silence" does not extend to give such a
           defendant as a matter of right the same protection in contemporaneous civil
           proceedings. The plaintiff in a civil action is not debarred from pursuing action in
           accordance with the normal rules merely because to do so would, or might, result
           in the defendant, if he wished to defend the action, having to disclose, in resisting
           an application for summary judgment, in the pleading of his defence, or by way of
           discovery or otherwise, what his defence is likely to be in the criminal proceeding
           (ibid at 904–5);

       (i) The court should consider whether there is a real and not merely notional danger
           of injustice in the criminal proceedings (ibid at 905);

       (j) In this regard factors which may be relevant include:
               (i) the possibility of publicity that might reach and influence jurors in the civil
               proceedings (ibid at 905);

               (ii) the proximity of the criminal hearing (ibid at 905);

               (iii) the possibility of miscarriage of justice eg by disclosure of a defence
               enabling the fabrication of evidence by prosecution witnesses, or interference
               with defence witnesses (ibid at 905);

               (iv) the burden on the defendant of preparing for both sets of proceedings
               concurrently (Beecee Group v Barton);
               (v) whether the defendant has already disclosed his defence to the
               allegations (Caesar v Somner at 932; Re Saltergate Insurance Co Ltd at 736);
               (vi) the conduct of the defendant, including his own prior invocation of civil
               process when it suited him (cf Re Saltergate Insurance Co Ltd at 735–6);
       (k) The effect on the plaintiff must also be considered and weighed against the effect
           on the defendant. In this connection I suggest below that it may be relevant to
           consider the nature of the defendant's obligation to the plaintiff;

       (l) In an appropriate case the proceedings may be allowed to proceed to a certain
           stage, eg, setting down for trial, and then stayed Beecee Group v Barton).

       (Citations of authorities mentioned omitted).

C.    Legal Principles – Adjournments
21.   The Tribunal has powers to adjourn matters pursuant to s 82 of the Administrative Review
      Tribunal Act 2024 (Cth) (ARTA). The Tribunal will not adjourn a hearing date unless it
      considers that there are good reasons to justify the adjournment 10. Where the Tribunal
      adjourns a matter, it will seek to re-list the matter as soon as practicable afterwards 11.
22.   The powers of the Tribunal must be considered in light of the objectives of the Tribunal,
      which require that applications to the Tribunal are resolved as quickly and with as little
      formality and expense as the proper consideration of the matter before the Tribunal
      permits 12.
23.   There is no automatic right to a stay of civil proceedings merely because concurrent criminal
      proceedings are pending 13. While it may be appropriate for the Tribunal to adjourn a matter
      where concurrent criminal proceedings arise out the same circumstances, there can be no
      basis for an adjournment where no criminal proceedings have been instituted, and no issues
      are under active consideration for criminal proceedings 14.
24.   The delay of proceedings for an indeterminate period amount of time amounts to a refusal
      to hear those proceedings 15.

E.    Legal Principles – Severance of proceedings
51.   The principles relevant to an application to separate parties to a proceeding are set out in
      detail in Australian Securities and Investments Commission v Axis International Management Pty
      Ltd [2009] FCA 250 at [9] – [13]:
         [9] The relevant principles are not controversial. The basic principle, which ought to
         guide the exercise of discretion, is that “the court should take whatever course seems to
         be most conducive to a just resolution of the disputes between the parties, but having
         regard to the desirability of limiting, so far as practicable, the costs and delay of the
         litigation”: Bishop v Bridgelands Securities (1990) 25 FCR 311 at 314. This case involved an
         application for joinder of additional applicants, which raises similar considerations to the
         present one.
         [10] If inconvenience is alleged, it must be shown that there are factors which make it
         inconvenient for there to be a joint trial, rather than that a separate trial is more
         convenient: Mann v Board of Health (ACT) (1996) 67 FCR 383 at 399F.
         [11] Disadvantages to a defendant seeking a separate trial, including the possibility of
         unfairness, must be weighed against identified advantages to ASIC and to the defendants
         as a whole and the efficient use of the court’s resources having regard to the commonality
         of issues raised by each claim: Dean-Willcocks v Air Transit International Pty Ltd (2002) 55
         NSWLR 64 at [34]. The court should be concerned to determine what is the most efficient
         use of the resources of the parties and also of the court: Carter v Commissioner of
         Taxation (2001) 109 FCR 215 at [23].
         [12] Factors which have been held to weigh in favour of exercising the discretion to order
         separate trials include:
         (a)   the issues concerning the party seeking the separate trial are distinct and
               discrete: Bishop v Bridgeland Securities at 314;
         (b)   a joint trial will involve the respondent in the expense of being present at the trial
               of questions with which they are not concerned and it would be unfair for the
               respondent to be compelled to participate in a large and costly proceeding where it
               has only marginal involvement: Hinze v Zed [1926] SASR 77 at 84; Knight v Beyond
               Properties Pty Ltd (No 2) [2006] FCA 192 at [30] and Newman v Hold Pty Ltd [2001]
               VSC 282 at [17].

              SUPPLEMENTARY SUBMISSIONS OF THE RESPONDENT

Introduction and Overview
1.   By email on 8 March 2026, the applicant in each of these three related applications
     lodged further submissions with the Tribunal ahead of the case management hearing
     listed for 20 March 2026. In their further submissions:

     1.1. Ms Vida Reid confirmed that she would no longer be pressing her application to
          have her proceeding stayed;

     1.2. Ms Vida Reid confirmed that she still seeks to have her proceeding severed from
          those of Mr Simon Reid and Forward Focused Group Pty Ltd (FFG);

     1.3. Mr Simon Reid pressed an application to have his proceeding severed from those
          of Ms Vida Reid and FFG;

     1.4. Mr Simon Reid, as director of FFG, pressed an application to have FFG’s
          proceeding delayed until after the proceedings of Ms Vida Reid and Mr Simon Reid
          have been heard; and

     1.5. both Ms Vida Reid and Mr Simon Reid foreshadowed requests to file updated
          Statements of Facts, Issues and Contentions (SFIC) and requested that the
          respondent provide them with additional documents prior to the filing of these
          SFICs.

2.   By email dated 17 March 2026, the Tribunal wrote to the parties noting that Ms Vida
     Reid was no longer proceeding with her stay application and seeking the views of the
     parties as to whether the hearing on Friday 20 March 2026 to hear the interlocutory
     application to sever the joint proceedings for matters 2023/3361 and 2023/3363 be
     heard on the papers.

Severance of proceedings

4.    The respondent continues to rely primarily on its submissions dated 17 September 2025
      on the issue of severance. 1 The following submissions are made by way of response
      and for completeness only.

5.    Ms Vida Reid’s new submissions state that her application raises issues that are distinct
      from those raised in Mr Simon Reid’s and FFG’s proceedings, and which warrant
      severance from the other proceedings. 2 In particular, she has foreshadowed
      submissions relating to:

      5.1. whether the original notice of intention in relation to Ms Vida Reid’s banning order
           was validly served in time to give her an opportunity to properly respond; and

      5.2. whether, during the Respondent’s internal review of Ms Vida Reid’s banning order,
           Ms Reid had a proper opportunity to respond to the delegate’s proposal to amend
           the banning order. 3

6.    The respondent submits that these submissions are misconceived. The review before
      the Tribunal is not a judicial review, but rather a merits review. Ms Vida Reid will be
      provided the opportunity to provide a response relating to the banning order at the final
      hearing. The respondent submits that this does not provide a basis for Ms Vida Reid’s
      proceeding to be severed from that of Mr Simon Reid or FFG.

7.    Ms Vida Reid further states that her application should not be delayed by interlocutory
      disputes arising in the related matters. 4 It is not clear what interlocutory disputes are
      referred to by this submission, given that the main interlocutory dispute to date has been
      Ms Reid’s own stay application (which she has now abandoned). In any event, for the
      reasons set out at paragraphs 8 and 9 below, the respondent submits that this
      submission should not be accepted.

8.    Mr Simon Reid’s submissions similarly state that his matter should not be delayed by
      interlocutory matters in the related matters. 5 The respondent, similarly, submits that this
      submission should not be accepted. As noted above, Ms Vida Reid has now abandoned
      her stay application. FFG has to date raised no separate interlocutory issues for
      consideration. As such, there are no further interlocutory matters on foot which could
