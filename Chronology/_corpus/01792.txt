# Draft Statement of Claim

**Federal Court of Australia — Western Australia District Registry**  
**Simon Reid and Vida Reid v Commissioner of the NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission**

## A. Parties and jurisdiction

1. The First Applicant, Simon Reid, is an individual residing in Western Australia.

2. The Second Applicant, Vida Reid, is an individual residing in Western Australia.

3. The Respondent is the Commissioner of the NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission appointed under the *National Disability Insurance Scheme Act 2013* (Cth) (the Act).

4. At all material times, each delegate who made or reviewed an impugned decision was an officer of the Commonwealth.

5. The matter arises under the Act and concerns relief against an officer of the Commonwealth.

6. The Court has jurisdiction under s 39B(1) and (1A)(c) of the *Judiciary Act 1903* (Cth), and power to grant complete declaratory and consequential relief under ss 21–23 of the *Federal Court of Australia Act 1976* (Cth).

## B. Statutory scheme

7. At each material date, s 73ZN(2) of the Act empowered the Respondent, by written notice, to make a banning order prohibiting or restricting a person from engaging in specified activities if a condition in s 73ZN(2)(a) was satisfied.

8. Relevantly, those conditions included that the Respondent:

    a. reasonably believed that the person had contravened, was contravening or was likely to contravene the Act; or

    b. reasonably believed that the person was not suitable to be involved in the provision of supports or services to people with disability.

9. Section 73ZN(3) permitted an order of general or limited application, permanently or for a specified period, and on specified conditions, but did not displace the requirement that the order prohibit or restrict the named person’s engagement in specified activities.

10. Before making an order, the statutory scheme ordinarily required a reasonable opportunity to make submissions on the matter.

11. Under s 100 of the Act, an internal reviewer could confirm, vary or set aside the original decision and make a substitute decision, but was required to exercise that review function according to law and procedural fairness.

12. Contravention of a banning order carried serious statutory consequences. A valid order therefore had to identify its prohibited activities with sufficient clarity to be understood and obeyed.

## C. Simon Reid — original decision

13. On 21 December 2022, Philip Halton, described as Branch Head Operations NCW and a delegate of the Respondent, made a decision under s 73ZN(2)(a)(iii) to make a banning order against the First Applicant.

14. The written order stated:

> Pursuant to section 73ZN(2)(a)(iii) of the Act, I prohibit Mr Reid from providing disability supports and services to NDIS-funded participants for a period of five (5) years, from the date that this order takes effect.

15. The order took effect at 5:00 pm AWST on 21 December 2022.

16. Before that decision, a Commission investigation record stated:

> Despite emails for FFG and The Hive being directed to or including Simon Reid the Commission has limited information about his actual involvement in the operations.

17. The executive briefs later relied upon by the original decision-maker identified, as the principal personal bridges to the alleged corporate conduct, that the First Applicant:

    a. held a position of responsibility or authority over Hybrid;

    b. was a joint bank signatory; and

    c. was said likely to have benefited from funds paid to corporate accounts.

17A. The Commission's Banning Order Policy in the decision record stated that a member of an NDIS provider's key personnel would be unlikely to receive a banning order unless directly involved in the conduct of concern or unless the person failed to take appropriate steps for which that person was responsible in a way that significantly facilitated, contributed to or exacerbated the conduct.

18. The original decision did not identify, for each activity prohibited by the order:

    a. an act, omission, state of knowledge or personal attribute of the First Applicant;

    b. an objectively reasonable basis for believing that the First Applicant’s engagement in that activity created an unacceptable risk;

    c. why any such risk could not be adequately mitigated by conditions or a narrower restriction; or

    d. why that risk required a five-year prohibition.

## D. Simon Reid — review decision and varied order

19. The First Applicant sought internal review on 25 January 2023.

20. On 21 April 2023, Sian Leathem, Complaints Commissioner and a delegate of the Respondent, decided to vary the original order.

21. The varied written order stated:

> Pursuant to section 73ZN(2)(a)(iii) of the NDIS Act, I prohibit Simon Reid from having any involvement with or in the NDIS sector, in any capacity, for a period of five (5) years. This includes Simon Reid being prohibited from:

22. The order then identified three inclusive examples, namely:

    a. providing disability supports and services, directly or indirectly, to NDIS-funded participants;

    b. providing labour hire workers to support NDIS providers; and

    c. providing support, funding, guidance or assistance of any form to an NDIS provider.

23. The varied order took effect at 12:00 am AWST on 22 April 2023 and stated that it would remain in force until 12:00 am AWST on 22 December 2027.

24. In paragraph 38 of the review reasons, the reviewer stated:

> It is appropriate to attribute liability to Mr Reid for the wrongdoing of the corporate entity he directs and the conduct of the workers employed or otherwise engaged by that company.

24A. Before adopting that reasoning, the reviewer found that parts of the original case could not reasonably or appropriately be attributed to the First Applicant because of the periods in which he held relevant responsibility, gave minimal weight to the approximately $1.485 million expired-plan figure because the Tactical Intelligence Report did not establish what proportion concerned services actually provided, and corrected instances of misattribution or double counting.

25. The reviewer relied upon director duties, corporate positions, signatory status, alleged benefit, and later material concerning Dream Big Employment Pty Ltd and Vitality WA Pty Ltd.

25A. The later administrative material included an email which the reviewer treated as sent by the First Applicant on 9 February 2023 from `simon@vitalitywa.au` to numerous recipients, with the subject “employment Oppurtunities in the disability sector”, and as seeking to recruit disability support workers for Vitality.

25B. On 22 March 2023, the reviewer notified the First Applicant that she proposed, taking that and other new material into account, to broaden the order expressly to include providing labour hire workers to support NDIS providers and providing support, funding, guidance or assistance of any form to an NDIS provider.

25C. At paragraph 42(b) of the review reasons, the reviewer described Vitality as a company providing labour hire workers to NDIS providers. At paragraph 43, she treated Vitality and Dream Big as possible strategic or tactical circumvention and stated that it was necessary to broaden the order accordingly.

25D. The original personal order did not prohibit recruitment, labour hire or sector involvement as such. The review reasons did not identify how sending the email breached the text of the original personal order.

25DA. The Dream Big company application upon which the reviewer relied was submitted on 29 October 2022, before the First Applicant received the 17 November 2022 notice of intention and before the original order was made on 21 December 2022.

25DB. On 20 January 2023, the Respondent issued Dream Big a notice under s 56 of the NDIS Act which stated that the Respondent was investigating, among other things, whether the First Applicant was providing supports or services in contravention of the original order.

25DC. Dream Big's written response stated that the First Applicant submitted the ASIC form, had no involvement in Dream Big's ongoing operations or service delivery, and was not one of its bank signatories. The response identified Virginie Fanny-Dulloo and Tori Fitton as the only bank signatories.

25DD. In an examination conducted by Commission officers on 9 March 2023, Ms Fanny-Dulloo stated, in substance, that the First Applicant was not involved in FFG, had helped with Dream Big's ASIC registration before he was banned, had no access to Dream Big's email accounts, and had no ongoing operational role.

25DE. The review reasons' list of materials identified the Dream Big application, the Vitality email and the Burke statement. It did not identify the s 56 written response or the 9 March 2023 examination.

25DF. The review reasons nevertheless stated that the First Applicant's involvement in Dream Big seemed to have commenced around the time the original order was made and treated the establishment of Dream Big as indicating possible strategic or tactical circumvention.

25E. The email and the reviewer’s reasons identified, at their highest, a specific labour-recruitment and labour-supply concern for which a targeted restriction was available. They did not identify a risk-responsive basis for prohibiting every other capacity stated or captured by the varied order.

26. The reviewer concluded that the First Applicant was “not suitable at this time to be an NDIS provider”, but the order was not confined to being a provider, executive, key personnel, financial controller or portal user.

27. The reviewer said the five-year duration corresponded with the time expected for regulatory and legal processes to conclude.

27A. Vida Reid is the First Applicant’s wife and an NDIS participant. Alex Reid is their son and an NDIS participant.

27B. The First Applicant has family and caring responsibilities concerning Vida’s and Alex’s supports, including ordinary unpaid assistance in attending to their NDIS-related needs.

27C. Neither the original nor the varied Simon order includes an exception for the First Applicant to seek, arrange, attend to or communicate about supports or services for Vida or Alex.

27D. The Second Applicant’s varied order, made by the same reviewer on the same day, expressly permits her to seek disability supports or services for herself and Alex.

27E. The First Applicant cannot identify from the words “any involvement with or in the NDIS sector, in any capacity” and “assistance of any form” whether ordinary unpaid family, caring, advocacy, nominee or provider-communication activities are prohibited.

## E. Vida Reid — original decision

28. On 20 December 2022, Mr Halton, as delegate, made a decision under s 73ZN(2)(a)(i) to make a banning order against the Second Applicant.

29. The written order stated:

> Pursuant to section 73ZN(2)(a)(i) of the NDIS Act, I prohibit Vida Reid from providing supports and services, directly or indirectly, to NDIS-funded participants for a period of five (5) years from the date that this order takes effect.

30. The order took effect at 5:30 pm AWST on 20 December 2022, subject to the exceptions stated in it concerning supports or services for the Second Applicant or her son.

31. The words “directly or indirectly” did not identify only activities in which the Second Applicant herself was prohibited from engaging and did not state a certain outer boundary.

## F. Vida Reid — review process, decision and varied order

32. The Second Applicant sought internal review on 25 January 2023.

33. The original decision proceeded under s 73ZN(2)(a)(i), on the footing of an actual, ongoing or likely contravention.

34. During review, a letter dated 22 March 2023 disclosed information concerning Dream Big and proposed broader wording and gave the Second Applicant an opportunity to respond to that information.

35. The 22 March 2023 letter did not fairly disclose that the reviewer proposed to:

    a. abandon or displace s 73ZN(2)(a)(i) as the decisive statutory condition;

    b. determine the matter instead under s 73ZN(2)(a)(iii), as a personal-suitability case;

    c. treat substantial submissions directed to the alleged contraventions as unnecessary to the dispositive reasoning; or

    d. apply a stated “usual practice” concerning paragraph (iii) unless a compelling reason to depart from it was shown.

36. On 21 April 2023, Ms Leathem decided to vary the Second Applicant’s original order and proceeded under s 73ZN(2)(a)(iii).

37. The varied order stated:

> Pursuant to section 73ZN(2)(a)(iii) of the NDIS Act, I prohibit Vida Reid from having any involvement with or in the NDIS sector, in any capacity, for a period of five (5) years. This includes Vida Reid being prohibited from:

38. The order then used the same three inclusive examples pleaded at paragraph 22.

39. The varied order took effect at 12:00 am AWST on 22 April 2023 and stated that it would remain in force until 12:00 am AWST on 21 December 2027.

40. At paragraph 41 of the review reasons, the reviewer treated detailed examination of substantial submissions concerning alleged contraventions as not necessary or conducive because the review was determined on personal suitability under paragraph (iii).

40A. At paragraph 46, the reviewer nevertheless stated that an administrative banning decision required satisfaction on the balance of probabilities that the Second Applicant had engaged in the alleged conduct, and stated that she was so satisfied. The change of statutory frame therefore affected the examination given to responsive submissions while an affirmative adverse finding on the underlying conduct was retained.

41. At paragraphs 56–59, the reviewer referred to a “usual practice” of proceeding under paragraph (iii) pending criminal charges and concluded that there was no compelling reason to diverge from it.

42. The reviewer said the five-year duration corresponded with the time then expected for the criminal proceedings to conclude.

43. Had the Second Applicant been given fair notice of the decisive paragraph (iii) case, she would have sought clear particulars, a reasonable opportunity to obtain advice, and an opportunity to address separately the activities, conditions, safeguards and duration proposed under that statutory basis.

44. There was a realistic possibility that the omitted opportunity could have affected the statutory condition selected or the order’s activities, conditions or duration, including because the reviewer:

    a. treated substantial contravention submissions as unnecessary or not conducive under the substituted basis;

    b. imposed a universal, non-exhaustive sector prohibition; and

    c. demonstrated in the same decision that exceptions and narrower drafting were available.

44A. The Applicants rely on the Commission’s notices, evidence and reasons as parts of the administrative record. They do not adopt any allegation, characterisation or inference merely by pleading what the record contained.

## G. Common Ground 1 — orders not confined to specified activities

45. Paragraphs 1–44 are repeated.

46. Properly construed, s 73ZN(2) authorised a prohibition or restriction on the named person engaging in specified activities.

47. It did not authorise a prohibition on:

    a. any involvement with an entire sector in any capacity;

    b. indirect involvement as such;

    c. an undefined relationship of support, funding, guidance or assistance “of any form”; or

    d. activities outside a non-exhaustive list introduced by “includes”.

48. The Simon and Vida varied orders purport to do each of those things.

49. Their chapeau and examples create overlapping or competing boundaries and do not tell the Applicants the whole of the conduct prohibited.

50. The Simon and Vida varied orders are not orders of the kind authorised by s 73ZN(2), are beyond power, and are invalid.

51. The Vida original order is separately beyond power and invalid because it purports to regulate providing supports or services “indirectly”, rather than prohibiting the Second Applicant’s engagement in identified activities.

**Particulars**

The Applicants rely on *Sunflower Care Services Pty Ltd v Commissioner of the NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission (No 2)* [2025] FCA 1442 at [127]–[141].

## H. Common Ground 2 — misconstruction and legal unreasonableness

52. Paragraphs 1–51 are repeated.

53. The review power required a statutory and rational connection between:

    a. the activities specified;

    b. the particular Applicant and any attributes creating risk if the Applicant engaged in them;

    c. why the risk was unacceptable and could not adequately be mitigated by another response; and

    d. the breadth, duration and conditions of the order.

54. Each reviewer instead treated a general conclusion about suitability or sector participation as sufficient to prohibit every capacity stated in the order.

55. The reasons did not expose an evident and intelligible justification for extending every retained finding to direct supports, indirect conduct, labour hire, funding, guidance, assistance of any form, and every other form of sector involvement captured by the chapeau.

55A. In Simon’s case, the reviewer’s new evidence identified a specific labour-hire concern and thereby demonstrated the availability of a materially narrower activity-based response. It did not justify the universal chapeau or the remaining unrelated capacities.

55B. The absence of any family-support exception for Simon, despite the express exception in Vida’s same-day order, further demonstrates that the breadth of Simon’s order was not calibrated to the activities posing the alleged protective risk.

56. The reasons did not expose an evident and intelligible justification for a five-year duration by reference to protective need, as distinct from the expected timing of external processes.

57. Each review decision was made on a misconstruction of s 73ZN(2) and/or was legally unreasonable and affected by jurisdictional error.

**Particulars**

The Applicants rely on *Sunflower* at [180]–[191] and [202]–[209], and *Minister for Immigration and Citizenship v Li* [2013] HCA 18 at [76].

## I. Ground 3 — Vida procedural fairness

58. Paragraphs 28–44 are repeated.

59. Procedural fairness required the Second Applicant to know the critical issue on which the review might turn and have a meaningful opportunity to provide evidence and submissions on it.

60. General notice of Dream Big, proposed broader wording, the statutory scheme, or underlying factual allegations was not fair notice that paragraph (i) would be displaced by paragraph (iii) and that contravention submissions would be treated as largely unnecessary.

61. The reviewer denied the Second Applicant that opportunity.

62. The denial was material because it deprived the Second Applicant of a realistic possibility of a different outcome in the sense pleaded at paragraphs 43–44.

63. The review decision was affected by jurisdictional error and is invalid.

**Particulars**

The Second Applicant relies on *Nathanson v Minister for Home Affairs* [2022] HCA 26 at [1]–[2] and the evidence in her affidavit.

## J. Ground 4 — Vida failure to exercise independent statutory judgment

64. Paragraphs 28–44 are repeated.

65. Alternatively, the reviewer treated the stated usual practice as supplying a presumptive result unless the Second Applicant established a compelling reason to depart from it.

66. In doing so, the reviewer failed independently to determine on the Second Applicant’s individual facts whether paragraph (iii) was satisfied and what order, if any, was warranted.

67. The review decision was thereby made on a misconstruction of the power, by unlawful fettering, and/or in a legally unreasonable manner.

## K. Ground 5 — Simon personal and objectively reasonable statutory basis

68. Paragraphs 13–27 are repeated.

69. A power conditioned on the Respondent reasonably believing that the First Applicant was not suitable required the actual belief and facts and circumstances constituting objectively reasonable grounds for that belief.

70. Corporate office, signatory status, association, inferred benefit and director obligations could be relevant evidence, but did not create a statutory rule attributing all corporate or worker wrongdoing to the First Applicant personally.

70A. The Commission's own policy distinguished a key person's corporate status from direct involvement or a responsible failure that significantly facilitated, contributed to or exacerbated the conduct. It did not state that directorship or constructive awareness alone established personal unsuitability for all activities.

71. The investigation record disclosed a material gap concerning the First Applicant’s actual operational involvement.

72. The decision-makers did not identify primary material closing that gap by establishing the First Applicant's own act, omission, knowledge, authorisation or receipt in relation to each material alleged wrongdoing and each prohibited activity.

72AA. The reviewer's correction of attribution periods, double counting and the qualified expired-plan amount demonstrated that the original personal bridge required re-examination. The reviewer then treated directorship and what the First Applicant “should have been aware” of as sufficient to attribute corporate and worker wrongdoing.

72A. On the administrative record, the reviewer treated the 9 February 2023 email as personal evidence relevant to a possible labour-hire restriction. The Applicants do not adopt the reviewer’s characterisation of the email. The error alleged is not that the reviewer was forbidden to consider material before her. It is that the reviewer treated that material as supporting an uncertain prohibition on every sector involvement and did not address a targeted labour-hire restriction as the natural narrower response.

72B. The conduct evidenced by the email was not prohibited by the original Simon order. The reviewer could assess it prospectively as new risk material but could not treat a later variation as retrospectively making the earlier conduct unlawful. The “circumvention” conclusion did not supply the missing statutory justification for the whole-sector chapeau or the unrelated capacities: *Sunflower* at [226].

72BA. The Dream Big application pre-dated the original notice and order. The Respondent's own intervening s 56 process produced evidence denying ongoing First Applicant involvement, bank authority and email access. The review reasons did not expose consideration of that material or explain how pre-order incorporation assistance rationally evidenced attempted circumvention of an order not yet made.

72BB. In those circumstances, the reviewer's Dream Big conclusion was affected by a material misunderstanding of the chronology and evidentiary record, lacked an evident and intelligible justification, and/or did not provide objectively reasonable grounds for the universal varied order.

73. At paragraph 38 of the review reasons, the reviewer expressly used attributed liability for corporate and worker wrongdoing as a substitute for the required personal statutory assessment.

74. By doing so, and by imposing an undifferentiated prohibition on all supports and services and later all sector involvement, the original and review decision-makers:

    a. misconstrued s 73ZN(2)(a)(iii);

    b. failed to form the statutory belief on objectively reasonable personal grounds; and/or

    c. exercised the power in a legally unreasonable manner.

75. The original and review decisions were affected by jurisdictional error and are invalid.

**Particulars**

The First Applicant relies on *Prior v Mole* [2017] HCA 10 at [4], *Sunflower* at [180]–[191] and [202]–[209], T6, T9.4, EC22-002103, EC22-002429, EC22-002521, and paragraphs 28–40 of the review reasons.

The First Applicant also relies on *Sunflower* at [226], T44, T58, T65/T65.1 and paragraphs 41-43 of the review reasons.

## L. Discretion, ART proceedings and utility

76. On 10 May 2023, the Applicants applied to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal for merits review. Those matters became ART matters 2023/3363 (Simon) and 2023/3361 (Vida).

77. The merits-review proceedings remain undetermined as at the date of filing. In particular:

    a. on 8 March 2026, Vida withdrew her stay application but maintained her severance application, while Simon also sought severance;

    b. in submissions dated 19 March 2026, the Respondent acknowledged that Simon’s original order, EC22-002103, EC22-002429, EC22-002521 and other Simon-specific documents had not been included in the tender bundle filed on 13 June 2024;

    c. on 22 April 2026, the ART stated that no decision had yet been made on severance;

    d. on 7 May 2026, the Respondent provided the omitted Simon package and applied under s 70 of the *Administrative Review Tribunal Act 2024* (Cth) for non-disclosure of two legal-advice attachments;

    e. on 19 May 2026, the Registry advised that the s 70 application had been put before the Deputy President for consideration, and Simon emailed submissions opposing it later that day;

    f. on 24 June 2026, Simon requested confirmation, status updates and expected delivery dates concerning severance and the s 70 application; and

    g. as at 15 July 2026, no reply, severance outcome, s 70 outcome, later direction or substantive hearing listing had been communicated to the Applicants by email.

78. The Applicants have continuously disputed the orders, although responsibility for individual periods of delay is mixed and is addressed candidly in the affidavits.

79. On 21 November 2025, the Federal Court delivered *Sunflower*, authoritatively addressing the same statutory power and materially similar order wording.

80. The orders continue to operate and be published, affect the Applicants’ affairs, and expose them to enforcement under wording whose boundary is uncertain.

81. The orders expire in December 2027. Further delay risks making effective judicial relief practically hollow.

82. The Federal Court proceeding is confined to legality. The Applicants do not ask this Court to determine the correct or preferable merits outcome.

83. The ART proceedings do not retrospectively validate an instrument made beyond power and do not remove the present controversy about enforcement and publication.

84. The Court can avoid duplication by determining the short statutory and documentary issues expeditiously and coordinating directions with the ART matters.
