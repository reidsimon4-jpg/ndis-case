E. Procedural Flaws in the Commission’s Decision and Variation

The Applicant’s case on review is that the banning order (and its subsequent “variation”)
are invalid or should be set aside due to fundamental procedural flaws, irrespective of
the underlying facts. For the ART to conclude this it first needs to ensure fair justice and
to approve a stay of proceedings in favour of the applicant. The Respondent’s
submissions tellingly fail to engage with these flaws, instead conflating process with
merits. We address three key procedural issues in turn:

1. Lack of Procedural Fairness (Denial of a Fair Hearing)

fairness. Ms Reid was not afforded a genuine opportunity to know and respond to the
case against her before the Commission made its banning order. In particular:

F. Improper Reliance on Variation Powers (Ultra Vires “Variation” under s 73ZO)

1. The next process issue concerns the Commission’s use (or misuse) of its power to
vary a banning order under s 73ZO of the NDIS Act. The Applicant submits that the
Commission’s actions in “varying” her banning order went beyond a lawful variation
and amounted to a new substantive decision without due process.

      1.1 Change of Grounds: The original banning order issued to Ms Reid on 20
          December 2022 was expressly based on one set of grounds – reportedly
          under s 73ZN(2)(a)(i) of the Act, which relates to contraventions of the NDIS
          Act or Rules. However, on 21 April 2023, the Commission characterized its
          internal review outcome as a “variation” but in fact switched the legal
          basis of the ban to s 73ZN(2)(a)(iii), the “suitability grounds”. This shift from
          an alleged rule breach to an assertion of general unsuitability substantially
          broadened the scope of the banning order. In practical terms, what had
          been a ban tied to specific conduct (e.g. an alleged fraud against the NDIS
          rules) was morphed into a ban premised on Ms Reid’s overall character or
          fitness, and expanded to bar any involvement in the NDIS sector in any
          capacity (even indirect roles).
      1.2 Circumvention of Safeguards: Such a fundamental change in the basis of
          the decision could not lawfully be done under the guise of a mere
          “variation.” The Applicant maintains that this was in substance a new
          banning order dressed up as a variation – an act which circumvented the
          procedural safeguards and fairness that would ordinarily apply to a fresh
          decision. The Commission’s own correspondence acknowledges that this
          “appears to constitute a new decision rather than a genuine variation –
          potentially circumventing proper procedural protections.”. That
          acknowledgement (made in Ms Reid’s letter to the Commission) is telling. By
           switching grounds and enlarging the ban without restarting the decision-
           making process (e.g. issuing a new show-cause notice or giving Ms Reid a
           chance to respond to the new grounds), the Commission abused the s 73ZO
           power. Section 73ZO allows variations in certain circumstances, but it was
           not intended to permit a wholesale revision of the nature and rationale of a
           banning order, especially to the detriment of the affected person’s rights.
2 Lack of Clarity and Notice: Even if the Commission had power to vary, the manner
  in which it did so was procedurally flawed. Ms Reid was notified of the
  Commission’s “Intention to take into account new information and vary the banning
  order” only on 22 March 2023, with a very short deadline to respond by 5 April 2023.
  The “new information” in Attachment A concerned allegations of her involvement in
  another entity (Dream Big Employment Pty Ltd). The Commission then proceeded to
  implement the variation on 21 April 2023, presumably incorporating that new
  allegation. The Applicant submits that this process was rushed and failed to give her
  a reasonable opportunity to gather evidence and respond to the new, broadened
  allegations. Moreover, treating this as part of the internal review (rather than
  initiating a separate banning order on new grounds) denied Ms Reid the procedural
  rights she would have had if a new ban were proposed – such as a fresh notice
  under s 73ZN and a chance to respond specifically on the “suitability” issues in a
  proper timeframe. The internal review power should not be misused to engineer
  what is effectively a more sweeping ban without due process.

2.1 In summary, the Commission’s approach to varying the decision under s 73ZO is a
major procedural flaw that will be central to the Applicant’s case. The Respondent’s
submissions on the stay application did not substantively address this point at all. By
granting a stay, the Tribunal can ensure that Ms Reid is not forced to endure the effects
of a banning order tainted by an ultra vires variation, pending the full review of this
issue.

H. No Reliance on Untested Allegations to Deny Stay of proceedings

Ms Reid justifies refusing a stay of proceedings, the Applicant submits this is
misconceived in law and fact. In merits review proceedings, particularly where
procedural validity is in question, the seriousness of unproven allegations cannot
override an applicant’s right to a fair process:
•   Allegations Are Not Findings: The Commission’s allegations (fraud, improper
    control of funds, harassment of participants, etc.) remain just that – allegations.
    No court or tribunal has found Ms Reid guilty of fraud; to the contrary, the only

    likewise not been tested or upheld by any neutral adjudicator. The Respondent

    maintaining the ban without scrutiny. The AAT’s role is to review the decision,
    not to endorse the allegations on faith. Indeed, if the Commission’s process
