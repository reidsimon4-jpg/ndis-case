A. Overview and relief maintained
These submissions reply to the Respondent’s document dated 17 September 2025. The Applicant maintains the relief set out in her Application for Stay and Severance filed 16 August 2025, namely:
(a) a narrowly-tailored stay to protect procedural fairness pending clarification of whether any parallel law enforcement in the/at the request of/considered part of NDIS Taskforce or related investigative activity (across all relevant agencies) remains on foot that could prejudice these proceedings; and
(b) severance so that matter 2023/3361 is heard separately from 2023/3363 (Mr Simon Reid) and 2023/4494 (FFG), given distinct issues, conflicts and given President K McMillan KC states during last hearing on ---- that I am not able to make any decisions for FFG as Mr.Simon Reid is the current listed Director , and demonstrable prejudice in a joint hearing to a self-represented party.
The Respondent’s submissions mischaracterise both heads of relief and mischaracterise my application under s 41 of the AAT Act. I do not seek an indefinite adjournment of the substantive proceedings. My application is for a protective stay, strictly limited until two fairness issues are resolved:
(a) confirmation of whether parallel investigative activity is ongoing across government agencies, and
(b) clarification and safeguards regarding the Respondent’s statement of “ No investigations”
The Applicant does not seek an indefinite adjournment of the “substantive proceedings”; she seeks protective, time-limited case-management orders to ensure a fair hearing.
I also seek severance of my matter (2023/3361) from Simon Reid and FFG, to avoid structural prejudice from joint proceedings.
B. Mischaracterisation of the stay as an “adjournment of the substantive proceedings”
The Respondent recasts the Applicant’s request as an application to “adjourn” the merits. That is wrong in substance and effect. The stay sought is conditional and protective: it pauses only so long as is reasonably necessary for objective clarification of parallel investigative risk and for minimal fairness safeguards following a conceded privacy/confidentiality mishandling.
The Respondent says there is “no automatic right” to a stay and relies on authorities about civil proceedings continuing alongside criminal processes. The Applicant accepts there is no automatic stay. Her point is different: where real prejudice to fairness is reasonably apprehended—because investigative activity may still be alive (beyond a single agency), and because a serious privacy/confidentiality breach has undermined equality of arms—a short, structured stay is the orthodox mechanism to protect the integrity of the Tribunal’s process and fair justice.
The Respondent argues the Applicant can simply claim privilege against self-incrimination “at the hearing”. That elides the distinct prejudice here: the chilling of a self-represented party’s ability to present her case fully and fearlessly while unresolved investigative shadows persist. Privilege is a shield to specific questions; it is not a cure-all for systemic unfairness arising from opacity about multi-agency activity and prior mishandling of her confidential materials.
C. The “no investigation” assurance is inadequate: NDIA ≠ the Fusion Taskforce universe
The Respondent points to an NDIA-only assurance. That does not answer the Applicant’s central concern: the NDIS Fraud Fusion Taskforce involves many agencies beyond the NDIA. The ABF stops, device seizures/retention and “flag” assertions (including for family members) post-date the dismissal of charges and objectively suggest ongoing government interest unrelated to the NDIA alone.
The Respondent says it is “not the Commission’s responsibility” to obtain cross-agency clarity and that the Tribunal cannot require it. Respectfully, that is not the Applicant’s contention. The submission is that before compelling a self-represented litigant to proceed, the Tribunal should exercise its own case-management powers to require minimal sworn clarification so the merits hearing does not unwittingly trespass upon, or prejudice, any live investigative process and so the Applicant is not chilled in the conduct of her case. This is squarely within the Tribunal’s protective function.
To avoid any spectre of indeterminate delay, the Applicant proposes clear guardrails:
The ART and Deputy President K McMillan KC demand the Respondent file an affidavit identifying the agencies to whom enquiries were made (including AFP, ACIC and ABF) and all government agencies who are collectively part of the NDIS TASK FORCE , the questions asked, and the answers received, and stating whether there are any parallel law-enforcement/investigative activity relating to the subject-matter of these applications.

The Australian Border Force directive – affidavit required
My affidavit (January 2025) records that on 25 September 2024 at Perth Airport, ABF officers, including but not limited to Julian detained my family and I ; and told us that  “The NDIS, NDIS Commission and the NDIS Minister have asked us to search you every time you enter or leave the country.”
This was not speculation or hearsay. It was a direct statement from an identified ABF officer whose name appears in my materials before the Tribunal.
 We therefore request particulars as to: a. the basis for which our family were detained, searched and had our property seized; and b. with reference to s 186A of the Customs Act 1901, the basis for which the contents of our family electronic devices were searched and copied. In reference to the above, we refer to the decision of DPP (Cth) v Farmer (a Pseudonym) and Ors [2017] VSCA 292, in which Priest JA stated at [207]: “Recognising that the Customs Act 1901 is concerned with both the exaction of revenue and the control of prohibited or restricted matter, it is consistent with the purposes and objects of the Act, and with the ordinary and natural meaning of the term, to conclude that export means taking goods out of Australia with the intention of landing them at some place beyond Australian territory. To that extent, export has connotations of a one-way journey of goods from Australia to an ultimate destination in another place outside Australia. Ordinarily, conveying the personal effects of a passenger — reading glasses, watch, wedding ring, wallet, camera and the like — from the country with no intention that they be landed in another, could not properly be described as an export of those personal effects. In the present case it could not accurately be said that there was any evidence that could justify the conclusion that the telephone accompanying Farmer on 14 April 2013 was to be landed in another country, let alone that it might attract duty, or that it was, or contained, prohibited goods. In my opinion, therefore, the telephone was not ‘goods for export’, and hence was not ‘subject to the control of customs’. Accordingly, the officers’ powers under ss186 and 186A were not engaged.”
With reference to Farmer, please advise how the customs officers’ apprehension, searching, and possible copying of our clients’ devices falls within the scope of the Act. What breach of the Act or associated legislation were our family and I reasonably suspected to have committed?

D. The Respondent’s inconsistency and fairness: they sought a stay when it suited them
In 2023 the Respondent itself sought a stay because AAT proceedings could prejudice criminal processes (e.g., by witnesses giving evidence in the Tribunal). That position made sense then—and its logic still holds where multi-agency interest remains opaque and ABF actions continued months after charges were dismissed followed by investigators attending multiple individuals and ex staff members of FFG’s homes in January 2025 not long ago. The Respondent offers no cogent justification for reversing its stance other than a vague and narrow NDIA “assurance”. That inconsistency undermines their credibility and falls short of their Model Litigant obligations.
Consistency in public administration matters. A Model Litigant does not oppose protective case-management that it previously championed on materially connected facts. The Applicant seeks no tactical advantage—only the same caution the Respondent invoked when doing so suited its litigation posture.
E. Privacy/confidentiality mishandling and equality of arms
The Respondent trivialises the Applicant’s privacy/confidentiality complaint as “not a matter for the Tribunal”. Even if ultimate determination lies elsewhere, the procedural consequences for fairness here and now are for this Tribunal. A self-represented party’s confidential materials were disseminated beyond what was proper; the Respondent admits forwarding them on. That has practical impacts on equality of arms, litigation strategy, and the Applicant’s confidence to disclose and argue without inhibition.
The proportionate remedy is not complex: a brief protective stay coupled with quarantine and disclosure directions, e.g.:
The Respondent to file an affidavit identifying to whom the Applicant’s materials were disseminated, when, and for what purpose;
A direction that those materials be quarantined from any non-party and not used except for the purposes of these proceedings; and
Any necessary confidentiality/suppression orders to restore a level playing field.
F. Severance is necessary to avoid concrete prejudice in a joint hearing
A joint hearing prejudices me as a self-represented litigant:
I face conflicts between my rights and Mr Reid/FFG’s interests.
Evidence admissible against others risks contaminating the view of my separate case.
I am disadvantaged logistically and procedurally in multi-party proceedings.
Severance does not prevent efficiency. Evidence can be adopted across matters by transcript or document without compelling a joint trial.
The Respondent says overlap of issues/witnesses defeats severance. That puts efficiency ahead of justice and a right to a fair hearing. The Tribunal’s task is to take the course “most conducive to a just resolution”, balancing efficiency with fairness. Here, specific prejudice from joinder has been clearly articulated:
(a) Conflicts and testimonial dilemmas: In a joint hearing, the Applicant’s decision not to give evidence (or to confine issues) can trigger adverse forensic consequences for Mr Reid. That places the Applicant in an impossible position—testify (and expose herself unnecessarily) to protect someone else’s case, or exercise her rights and prejudice him. Severance removes this coercive dynamic.
(b) Distinct issues and timeframes: The Applicant ceased as FFG director before certain events; her case includes unique suitability/fairness points (including a shift in statutory footing of her ban on internal review) that are irrelevant to Mr Reid/FFG. Joinder risks contamination—evidence admissible against one being psychologically (if not legally) weighed against the other.
(c) Self-represented disadvantage in multi-party trial: In a three-cornered contest, cross-examination sequencing, objections, and evidentiary rulings become exponentially harder for a self-represented person. That is not abstract; it is structural prejudice. Severance streamlines the Applicant’s matter into a conventional two-party merits review she can meaningfully conduct.
Any efficiency concerns can be mitigated with practical tools:
Where appropriate, the Tribunal can receive common factual evidence on the papers and adopt it across proceedings, or have witnesses called once with transcript adopted in the severed matter(s);
Directions can ensure no witness is needlessly recalled, and any overlap is managed by document use rather than compelled joint trial.
The balance, properly struck, favours severance: the prejudice to fairness in a joint hearing is concrete; the administrative inconvenience of managing two shorter, cleaner hearings is modest and manageable.
G. The “we cannot vary the ban” point misses the fairness issue now
The Respondent says the Commissioner presently “has no power” to vary the bans while review is on foot. Whether or not that is correct under the current ART scheme, it does not answer the Applicant’s fairness point: the internal review rationale (which the Respondent holds) explicitly tethered unsuitability to pending criminal charges and ongoing investigations; those charges were dismissed well over a year ago. The Respondent’s unwillingness to re-evaluate its position in light of that fundamental change is a live fairness consideration for this Tribunal when deciding whether protective case-management is warranted.
