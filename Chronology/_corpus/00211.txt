MRS VIDA REID — ULTRA VIRES VARIATION OF BANNING ORDER UNDER s 100 NDIS ACT
ART matter 2023/3361  |  Re Commissioner of the NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission

1.  The proposition in one sentence
On internal review under s 100 of the National Disability Insurance Scheme Act 2013 (Cth), the Complaints Commissioner purported to 'vary' Mrs Reid's banning order by switching its statutory foundation from s 73ZN(2)(a)(i) (contravention) to s 73ZN(2)(a)(iii) (suitability), without giving Mrs Reid notice that suitability was the new dispositive issue and without giving her an opportunity to address it. That was procedural unfairness within the meaning of Kioa v West (1985) 159 CLR 550 and within the specific rule stated by the High Court in SZBEL v Minister for Immigration and Multicultural and Indigenous Affairs [2006] HCA 63; (2006) 228 CLR 152.
2.  The relevant facts
(a)  On 19 November 2022 the Commission issued Mrs Reid with a Notice of Intention to make a banning order. The Notice identified the proposed statutory ground as s 73ZN(2)(a)(i) — that Mrs Reid had contravened the NDIS Act or NDIS Rules. The conduct particulars and the Statement of Reasons proceeded on that footing.
(b)  Mrs Reid received the Notice on 7 December 2022 (18 days after posting; 7 days after the stated response deadline of 30 November 2022). Through her then solicitors Tom & Co Legal, written submissions were filed on 30 November 2022 responding to the contravention ground.
(c)  On 20 December 2022 the Commission made the banning order against Mrs Reid. The order was made on s 73ZN(2)(a)(i) — contravention. The case Mrs Reid had been required to meet was the contravention case.
(d)  On 21 April 2023 the Complaints Commissioner issued internal review decision IR(6) 22/23. That decision purported to vary Mrs Reid's banning order under s 100. In substance, the variation re-anchored the order on a different statutory ground — s 73ZN(2)(a)(iii) (suitability). The contravention finding was materially discounted (and on the parallel IR(7) decision the same internal reviewer expressly discounted the central financial allegation at paragraph 29(j) by reference to the Tactical Intelligence Report).
(e)  At no point between 19 November 2022 and 21 April 2023 was Mrs Reid given a written notice that suitability under s 73ZN(2)(a)(iii) was a dispositive issue on which the Commission might make or sustain a banning order against her. She had no opportunity to address a suitability case. The 'variation' was the first occasion on which the suitability ground appeared as the operative basis for the order.
3.  The SZBEL mapping
In SZBEL v Minister for Immigration and Multicultural and Indigenous Affairs [2006] HCA 63; (2006) 228 CLR 152 the High Court held that where the decision-maker on review identifies a dispositive issue different from those that the primary decision-maker treated as dispositive, the affected person is entitled to (i) notice of the new dispositive issue and (ii) an opportunity to address it. The applicant is otherwise entitled to assume that the issues the primary decision-maker considered dispositive are the issues 'arising in relation to the decision under review'.
Applied to Mrs Reid:
  •  the primary decision-maker (the Commission) identified s 73ZN(2)(a)(i) (contravention) as the dispositive statutory issue;
  •  Mrs Reid was entitled to assume — on the SZBEL rule — that the issues 'arising in relation to the decision under review' were those identified at first instance, that is, contravention;
  •  the internal reviewer identified a different dispositive issue (suitability under s 73ZN(2)(a)(iii));
  •  the internal reviewer did not put Mrs Reid on notice that suitability was a new dispositive issue;
  •  the internal reviewer did not give Mrs Reid an opportunity to make submissions or adduce evidence on a suitability case;
  •  the variation was made on suitability without compliance with the SZBEL hearing rule.
On SZBEL, the variation is procedurally unfair and reviewable.
4.  The Kioa mapping
In Kioa v West (1985) 159 CLR 550 the High Court held that a person whose interests are adversely affected by a decision is entitled to procedural fairness as a matter of administrative law unless the statute clearly excludes it. The duty includes adequate notice of the case the person has to meet and a real (not merely formal) opportunity to respond. Mason J: notice must be adequate in substance, not merely in form. The relevant question is whether the affected person had a genuine, practical opportunity to respond — not merely that notice was given.
Applied to Mrs Reid:
  •  Mrs Reid's interests were adversely affected — a banning order materially restricts her ability to participate in the NDIS, with consequential effects on her profession, her business, her reputation and her insurance position;
  •  the statute does not clearly exclude procedural fairness on internal review under s 100 of the NDIS Act;
  •  Mrs Reid was given notice of one case (contravention) and a banning order was made on that case;
  •  the internal reviewer then sustained the banning order on a different case (suitability) — a different statutory ground, requiring different evidence and inviting different submissions — and gave Mrs Reid no notice and no opportunity to respond on that new case;
  •  the opportunity to be heard on the case ultimately relied on was, in Mason J's language, illusory.
On Kioa, the variation is procedurally unfair and the order as varied cannot stand.
5.  Why this matters in the ART
The ART conducts merits review and is required to come to its own view as to the correct or preferable decision under the merits-review framework in Shi v Migration Agents Registration Authority [2008] HCA 31; (2008) 235 CLR 286. The ART is not bound to defer to the Commission's internal review decision. On the SZBEL/Kioa analysis, the variation decision was made without affording procedural fairness on the new dispositive ground; the suitability case has therefore never been the subject of a procedurally regular determination at the Commission level. The correct or preferable decision available to the ART includes (i) setting aside the banning order as varied; (ii) remitting the matter to the Commission for fresh decision after proper procedural protections; or (iii) substituting a different decision on its own merits review.
Mrs Reid's position before the ART is therefore not that she must prove she is 'suitable' under s 73ZN(2)(a)(iii) — it is that the suitability case has never been put to her in a procedurally regular way, and the Commission cannot, by labelling a fresh decision a 'variation', cure that defect.
6.  Supporting authority
  •  Kioa v West (1985) 159 CLR 550 — foundation of the hearing rule in Australian administrative law.
  •  SZBEL v Minister for Immigration and Multicultural and Indigenous Affairs [2006] HCA 63; (2006) 228 CLR 152 — directly on point; review-level identification of new dispositive issues requires notice and an opportunity to respond.
  •  Re Minister for Immigration and Multicultural Affairs; Ex parte Lam (2003) 214 CLR 1 — where the conduct of the decision-maker has contributed to the failure of the affected person to make submissions on the operative case, the decision-maker cannot rely on the absence of submissions to assert that procedural fairness was satisfied.
  •  Shi v Migration Agents Registration Authority [2008] HCA 31; (2008) 235 CLR 286 — the merits-review framework: the ART steps into the shoes of the primary decision-maker and decides what the correct or preferable decision is on the material before it at the date of its own decision.
  •  Hot Holdings Pty Ltd v Creasy (1996) 185 CLR 149 — the right to be heard is undermined where the timeframe or framework provided makes meaningful response impossible.
