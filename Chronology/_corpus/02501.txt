﻿Resilient Transitional Infrastructure in Volatile Geologies: A Techno-Economic Framework for West Sumatra and Kalimantan Disaster Recovery (2026)
1. Strategic Context: The Convergence of Hydrometeorological Crisis and Geotechnical Instability
The disaster recovery landscape in Indonesia has entered a critical phase of transformation following the catastrophic hydrometeorological events of late 2025 and early 2026. The convergence of intensified monsoon patterns, driven by climate variability, and the inherent geological volatility of the archipelago—specifically the deep peatlands of Kalimantan and the seismically active, flood-prone valleys of West Sumatra—has exposed severe limitations in traditional post-disaster housing procurement.1 The paradigm that prioritized heavy, masonry-based reconstruction as the sole metric of "permanent" housing is faltering under the logistical and geotechnical realities of these specific disaster zones.
This white paper posits that the current crisis demands a shift from material-centric procurement to performance-centric engineering. Specifically, it advocates for the adoption of Lightweight Gauge Steel (LGS) modular systems elevated on helical screw piles as the primary intervention for swamp, peat, and flood-prone recovery zones. This is not merely an alternative construction method but a necessary adaptation to environments where the standard Rumah Instan Sederhana Sehat (RISHA) system faces critical performance risks due to soil subsidence and liquefaction.3
1.1 The 2025–2026 Sumatra Hydrometeorological Crisis
The end of 2025 marked a period of devastating geological instability for Sumatra. Triggered by Tropical Cyclone Senyar and exacerbated by La Niña conditions, flash floods and landslides swept through the regencies of Agam, Padang Pariaman, Tanah Datar, and South Pesisir.5 The sheer scale of the devastation was profound, with the National Disaster Management Agency (BNPB) reporting over 105,842 residents remaining in evacuation shelters as of early February 2026.6
The recovery effort faces a dual challenge: the volume of displacement and the degradation of the land itself. Reports indicate that approximately 147,000 houses were damaged across Aceh, North Sumatra, and West Sumatra.5 However, the reconstruction cannot simply replicate what was lost. The floods have left behind vast tracts of debris-laden, waterlogged soil and activated new landslide paths. In these zones, traditional shallow foundations are not only logistically difficult—requiring extensive dewatering and excavation—but are also prone to immediate settlement and failure. The government's target of 27,575 temporary housing units (Huntara) highlights the urgency, yet the deployment speed of traditional materials has lagged behind the humanitarian need.5
1.2 The Peatland Imperative in Kalimantan and Sumatra
While the immediate focus is on the flood recovery in West Sumatra, the broader national challenge involves housing stability in Indonesia’s vast peatlands. Indonesia possesses approximately 14.9 million hectares of peatland, much of which is located in Kalimantan and Sumatra.8 These ecosystems present a unique engineering hostility:
* High Compressibility: Peat functions as a sponge. The application of surface loads, such as a concrete slab or heavy masonry walls, compresses the organic matrix, expelling water and causing massive, often differential, settlement.4
* Low Shear Strength: The amorphous nature of peat means that traditional friction piles often fail to generate sufficient resistance unless driven to uneconomical depths.
* Chemical Aggression: Peat water is highly acidic (pH 3–4), which aggressively corrodes standard concrete and untreated steel reinforcements, compromising the structural integrity of permanent housing (Huntap) within years of construction.9
The failure of infrastructure in these zones is well-documented. Case studies from Riau and Central Kalimantan reveal that houses built on slab-on-grade foundations or short wooden piles often tilt, crack, and sink as the peat oxidizes and subsides—a process accelerated by drainage canals and land conversion.3 The degradation of these landscapes, further exacerbated by the failed Mega Rice Project of the 1990s and ongoing plantation concessions, creates a volatile substrate that rejects standard engineering solutions.3
1.3 Regulatory Framework: The Huntap vs. Huntara Dichotomy
The Indonesian government, through BNPB and the Ministry of Public Works and Housing (PUPR), operates under a bifurcated disaster housing policy that strictly delineates between temporary and permanent structures. Understanding this dichotomy is crucial for any NGO or government agency attempting to introduce high-performance transitional housing.
1.3.1 Hunian Sementara (Huntara)
* Strategic Purpose: To bridge the gap between emergency tents and permanent resettlement, providing dignified shelter for 6 months to 2 years.
* Financial Cap: The standard budget allocation is approximately IDR 30 million per unit.11
* Material Standard: Typically plywood, bamboo, or light steel frames with minimal finishing.
* Limitation: These units are often viewed as "disposable." In flood zones, however, a disposable unit that cannot be elevated is simply a future debris hazard. The IDR 30 million cap makes it nearly impossible to engineer a foundation capable of resisting flood scour or peat subsidence.
1.3.2 Hunian Tetap (Huntap)
* Strategic Purpose: Long-term resettlement for victims whose homes were heavily damaged or destroyed.
* Financial Cap: Approximately IDR 60 million per unit.11 This grant is intended to stimulate self-built housing (swakelola) or fund contractor-led mass construction.
* Material Standard: The default specification is the Type 36 (  ) reinforced concrete structure, often utilizing the RISHA panel system, with brick or panel walls.
* Limitation: The budget cap is derived from mass masonry economics in stable zones (like Java). It does not account for the "Logistics Premium" of remote disaster zones or the "Geotechnical Premium" required for deep foundations in swamps. Consequently, projects are often forced to compromise on the foundation to stay within budget, leading to the construction of permanent houses on impermanent ground.
This white paper identifies a "Missing Middle" in this framework: a High-Performance Transitional System that can be deployed as quickly as a Huntara but offers the structural safety and lifespan of a Huntap. The proposed LGS + Helical Pile system fills this gap, offering a solution that is technically superior for unstable ground, albeit at a price point that challenges current procurement models.

2. Technical Methodology: Elevated Modular Systems on Helical Piles
The core proposition of this methodology is the decoupling of the structure from the ground surface. By shifting from wet-trade heavy construction (cement, sand, water) to dry, prefabricated assembly, agencies can bypass the primary bottlenecks of disaster recovery: curing times, material scarcity, and site inaccessibility.
2.1 The Superstructure: Light Gauge Steel (LGS) Modular Units
Light Gauge Steel (Cold-Formed Steel) framing provides the optimal balance of rigidity, weight, and speed for disaster contexts.
   * Weight Reduction: A typical Type 36 LGS frame weighs between 1.0 to 1.5 tons, compared to the significantly higher dead load of a reinforced concrete RISHA frame.5 In peatlands, minimizing the overburden pressure is the single most effective strategy to reduce primary consolidation settlement.
   * Corrosion Resistance: The utilization of AZ150 (Aluminium-Zinc) coated steel offers superior resistance to the high humidity and acidic soil vapors of swamp environments compared to standard galvanized or black steel.14
   * Rapid Deployment: Factory-panelized frames allow for a "flatpack" logistics model. A single truck can transport the structural components for 5–10 houses, whereas transporting concrete panels or bricks for the same number would require a convoy. Assembly can be completed in 1–2 days by semi-skilled labor using torque guns, removing the 28-day curing bottleneck of concrete.5
2.2 The Foundation: The Engineering Necessity of Helical Screw Piles
The foundation is the pivot point of resilience. In the market, there is often dangerous confusion between "Solar Ground Screws" and "Helical Screw Piles." For residential occupancy in volatile soils, this distinction is a matter of life safety.
2.2.1 Solar Ground Screws: A Risk in Residential Applications
   * Geometry: Typically a tapered cone with continuous threading along the body, similar to a wood screw.
   * Load Mechanism: Relies primarily on skin friction (adhesion) between the soil and the screw surface.15
   * Intended Application: Designed for lightweight structures like solar racking (where wind uplift is the primary concern), fencing, and signage.17
   * Failure Mode in Disaster Zones:
   * Peat Punch-Through: In soft peat or swamp soils, skin friction is negligible. Under the vertical compression load of a house (even a light one), a ground screw can simply slip or "punch through" the soft layers, leading to catastrophic settlement.18
   * Heave Susceptibility: The continuous thread design allows expansive clays or frost (in other climates) to grip the pile body and heave it out of the ground.17
   * Installation Torque: Often installed with handheld electric drivers which cannot generate the torque required to verify high bearing capacities.18
2.2.2 Helical Screw Piles: The Required Standard for Habitation
   * Geometry: A slender central shaft (square or round) with one or more distinct helical plates welded near the tip.
   * Load Mechanism: Relies on end-bearing. The helical plates screw through the soft upper layers (peat/liquefiable sand) and embed into the deep, stiff soil strata (clay/dense sand). The plates act as a deep spread footing.15
   * Peat Performance: The slender shaft minimizes contact with the unstable upper peat, effectively "ignoring" the subsiding layer. The load is transferred strictly to the stable strata below.
   * Verifiable Capacity: The most critical advantage for disaster relief is Quality Assurance (QA). The load capacity of a helical pile is directly correlated to the installation torque. If the hydraulic drive head records a torque of 2,500 ft-lbs, the engineering capacity is mathematically guaranteed (e.g., via ICC-ES AC358 standards).5 This eliminates the need for time-consuming static load tests or concrete cylinder tests.
   * Adaptability: If the stable soil is 12 meters deep, modular extensions can be bolted on until the helix reaches the bearing strata. Solar screws generally do not offer this extendability.20
Technical Verdict: For Huntap and Huntara projects in flood or peat zones, helical screw piles are the only viable engineering choice. The use of solar ground screws for occupied housing in these soils presents an unacceptable risk of structural failure.
2.3 The "No-Concrete" Logistics Advantage
In the immediate aftermath of the West Sumatra floods, access to clean water for mixing concrete was severely limited. Furthermore, dewatering a foundation pit in a saturated swamp to pour a concrete footing is technically complex and cost-prohibitive.21
   * Helical Piles: Can be installed directly into waterlogged soil or standing water. No excavation is required. No dewatering pumps are needed. The pile can be loaded immediately after installation.22
   * Concrete Foundations: Require excavation, formwork, rebar placement, pouring, and a minimum curing period. In acidic peat environments, "green" concrete is vulnerable to chemical attack before it cures, necessitating expensive additives or protective liners.

3. Comparative Analysis: RISHA vs. LGS-Helical Systems
The Rumah Instan Sederhana Sehat (RISHA) is the incumbent technology promoted by the Ministry of PUPR. It utilizes a modular system of reinforced concrete panels and bolt connectors. While successful in stable regions like Lombok, its application in the peatlands of Kalimantan and the flood basins of Sumatra requires critical re-evaluation.
3.1 RISHA: The Incumbent Standard
   * Strengths:
   * Standardization: The system is widely codified in Indonesian National Standards (SNI), and local contractors are familiar with the assembly process.
   * Supply Chain: Components (panels, bolts) are often stockpiled by state-owned enterprises (BUMN) and can be produced by local MSMEs (UMKM).
   * Cost Alignment: The standardized pricing structure (~IDR 50 million for the structure) aligns perfectly with the government's IDR 60 million Huntap grant model.23
   * Weaknesses in Volatile Geologies:
   * Mass and Inertia: Being concrete, RISHA is heavy. In liquefaction zones (like Palu or Padang), this mass generates significant inertial forces during seismic events, increasing the demand on connections.24 In peatlands, the heavy dead load accelerates settlement.
   * Foundation Dependency: RISHA is purely a superstructure system. It requires a prepared foundation (typically stone masonry or a concrete slab). If the ground is a swamp, the RISHA system sits on a compromised base. The system does not solve the "ground problem".25
   * Logistical Weight: Each panel weighs approximately 50kg. A single house requires dozens of panels, necessitating heavy trucks and cranes or significant manual labor for transport, which is often impossible in severed disaster zones.25
3.2 LGS on Helical Piles: The Resilient Challenger
   * Strengths:
   * Foundation Integration: The pile is the foundation. It solves the geotechnical instability and the structural support requirement in a single operation.
   * Seismic Resilience: Steel is a ductile material; it can flex and absorb energy during earthquakes without catastrophic brittle failure. The high strength-to-weight ratio significantly reduces seismic inertial loads.26
   * Flood Resilience: The system is inherently designed to be elevated (the "stilt house" or Rumah Panggung concept). This keeps occupants and assets above floodwaters, reducing the need for recurring evacuations.5
   * Weaknesses:
   * Cost Barrier: The initial material cost of steel and engineered piles is significantly higher than local concrete and stone.
   * Skill Gap: Installation requires specialized hydraulic drive heads and trained crews to monitor torque, contrasting with the general masonry skills available in villages.5

4. Geotechnical Deep Dive: Peat Subsidence and Liquefaction Mechanics
4.1 Peatland Subsidence Mechanisms
In Sumatra and Kalimantan, peat layers can exceed 3 meters in depth. Understanding the mechanics of peat is essential for justifying the cost of helical piles.
   * Primary Consolidation: When a load (such as a house) is applied, water is squeezed out from the pore spaces of the peat matrix. This occurs relatively quickly and causes immediate settlement.
   * Secondary Compression (Creep): Over years and decades, the organic fibers within the peat decompose and reorganize. This causes long-term, continuous sinking.
   * The Helical Solution: By placing the load-bearing helical plates below the peat layer and into the firm mineral soil (clay/sand) underneath, the house is effectively "floating" above the compressing peat. The slender shaft minimizes the "downdrag" forces (negative skin friction) that would otherwise grab the sides of a standard friction pile and pull it down as the peat subsides.27
4.2 Liquefaction Resilience
The 2018 Palu earthquake demonstrated the devastation of soil liquefaction, a risk that remains high in parts of Sumatra.
   * Mechanism: During seismic shaking, saturated sandy soils lose their shear strength and behave like a liquid. Heavy surface structures (like concrete slabs) sink or tilt as the ground gives way.24
   * Helical Advantage: Helical piles provide tension (uplift) resistance. During the violent ground motion, the helices anchor the structure against uplift forces, while the deep embedment bypasses the liquefied surface layer. A lighter LGS house exerts less inertial force, further reducing the demand on the piles.28

5. Economic Analysis and Pricing Strategy
This section addresses the critical friction point between engineering necessity and fiscal constraint: The Price Gap.
5.1 The Cost Reality
Based on the "Infinity Living Options" white paper and market data, the turnkey prices for the LGS+Helical system are significantly higher than the government grants 5:
   * Option A (Rapid Shelter - 30m2): ~IDR 274,784,666
   * Option B (Family Unit - 30m2): ~IDR 398,625,040
   * Option C (Clinic/Command Hub): ~IDR 504,656,425
Constraint: The BNPB Huntap grant is strictly capped at IDR 60,000,000 for severely damaged homes.11 The Deficit: ~IDR 214,000,000 per unit (for the cheapest option).
5.2 Anatomy of the Gap
   1. Turnkey vs. Stimulus: The IDR 60m grant is often designed as a "stimulus" for self-built housing (swakelola). It covers basic materials but assumes free community labor and excludes land preparation costs. The LGS prices are commercial "Turnkey" rates including professional labor, profit, transport, and high-spec foundations.
   2. The Foundation Premium: A verified helical pile foundation for a 36m2 house (approx. 12 piles @ ~IDR 2.5m installed) costs IDR 30,000,000 alone.5 This single line item consumes 50% of the entire government housing budget.
   3. Material Spec: Imported or high-grade Galvalume LGS, insulation, and composite cladding drive costs higher than local red brick and tin roofing.

	5.4 Strategic Pricing Proposals for Adoption
To bridge the chasm between the IDR 60 million budget and the IDR 300+ million reality, we propose three specific procurement strategies for Government bodies and NGOs.
Strategy 1: The "Foundation-First" Intervention (The Hybrid Model)
   * Concept: The Government or Lead NGO tenders a contract solely for the Helical Pile Foundation Platform (~IDR 30-40 million).
   * Mechanism: The agency delivers a certified, elevated steel platform. The beneficiary then uses the remaining Huntap funds (IDR 20-30 million) + personal savings/labor to build the superstructure using local materials (timber, bamboo, or light brick) on top of this engineered platform.
   * Rationale: The state ensures the house won't sink (public safety/infrastructure), while the resident retains autonomy over the walls and roof. This effectively splits the budget: "Infrastructure Budget" covers the piles, "Housing Stimulus" covers the house.
Strategy 2: The "Core Unit" (Rumah Tumbuh)
   * Concept: Deploy a smaller, high-quality 18m2 "Core" unit (1 Room + Bathroom) on helical piles.
   * Cost Target: Reduce material volume to bring the cost closer to IDR 100 - 120 million.
   * Funding: BNPB provides the standard IDR 60m. An NGO partner (e.g., Habitat for Humanity, Red Cross) provides a "Top-Up Grant" of IDR 40-60m.
   * Rationale: Provides immediate, safe sanitation and shelter. Residents can expand the deck and add rooms later using traditional methods (wood/RISHA) as they recover financially.
Strategy 3: Critical Infrastructure Focus
   * Concept: Do not compete for mass individual housing. Position the fully-spec'd Option C (~IDR 500m) exclusively for Community Lifelines: Field Clinics (Pustu), Schools, and Mosques.
   * Rationale: These buildings must remain operational during floods to serve as command centers or shelters. The high cost is justified by the communal value. International donors (UN Agencies, World Bank) are the target funding source, not the individual housing grant.

6. Execution and Logistics Strategy
6.1 The "Kit-of-Parts" Supply Chain
The logistical paralysis in West Sumatra, where roads are often severed by landslides, demands a supply chain that does not rely on heavy haulage.
   1. Regional Stockpiling: Pre-position helical piles and LGS coils in regional hubs like Padang and Medan.
   2. Flatpack Efficiency: A single medium-duty truck (Colt Diesel) can carry the structural components for 10 flatpacked LGS houses, whereas it could only carry bricks/cement for 1-2 masonry houses.
   3. Last-Mile Agility: Helical piles (supplied in 1.5m or 2m modular sections) and steel framing members can be transported by small boats (klotok) or even motorcycles to remote villages that are inaccessible to cement mixers or cranes.
6.2 Installation Workflow
The speed of installation is the primary value driver, converting "months of displacement" into "days of recovery."
   1. Day 1: Geotechnical Scan: A lead team uses a handheld torque probe to map the depth to stable soil across the site.
   2. Day 1-2: Piling: A 3-man crew with a mini-excavator (or portable hydraulic power pack) installs 12 piles per unit. Crucial Step: The installation torque is logged for every pile to certify capacity.
   3. Day 3: Framing: The chassis is bolted to the pile caps. Wall and roof frames are erected using simple impact drivers.
   4. Day 4-5: Cladding & Fit-out: Pre-cut cladding panels are screw-fixed. Windows and doors are installed.
   5. Day 6: Handover and Occupancy.
Total Time: <1 Week per unit. (Compare to 4-8 weeks for masonry/RISHA due to foundation excavation, pouring, curing, and bricklaying).

7. Recommendations for Government and NGOs
7.1 For BNPB and PUPR
   * Official Recognition of "Peat Premium": The government must formally acknowledge that Huntap standards in peat/swamp areas require a higher budget coefficient (e.g., 1.5x or 2.0x the standard Java rate) to cover deep foundations. The "One Price Fits All" policy is technically flawed for the archipelago's diverse geology.
   * Mandate Torque Verification: Update procurement guidelines to mandate torque-monitoring logs for all foundations in liquefaction and peat zones. This provides a digital audit trail of safety that concrete testing cannot match in remote areas.
   * Pilot the "Foundation-First" Model: In the upcoming fiscal recovery phase, tender a contract specifically for "Elevated Foundation Platforms" using helical piles, separating the ground risk from the housing construction.
7.2 For NGOs (HFI, Red Cross, Wahana Visi, CRS)
   * Gap Funding Strategy: Instead of building a few expensive houses, focus donor funds on "Top-Up Grants" specifically for the foundation. If the government provides 60m, the NGO provides the 30m helical pile kit. This leverages government funds to ensure structural safety.
   * Target Vulnerable Sites: Deploy the LGS+Helical system specifically for riverbank communities and deep peat zones where relocation is impossible but elevation is mandatory. Leave the dry/stable ground sites for cheaper RISHA/masonry solutions.
7.3 For the Private Sector and Donors
   * Metric Shift: Shift impact reporting from "Number of Houses Built" to "Days to Occupancy." The LGS/Helical system creates immediate shelter, reducing the secondary public health crisis of prolonged camp habitation.
8. Conclusion
The reconstruction of West Sumatra and the development of Kalimantan cannot rely on the construction methods of Java. The geological reality—soft peat, rising floodwaters, and seismic faults—requires a system that floats above the hazard rather than fighting it.
While the Rumah Instan Sederhana Sehat (RISHA) remains a viable solution for stable ground, it is geotechnically inadequate for swamps without massive, slow, and expensive soil improvement. The Helical Pile + LGS Modular System offers the engineering resilience required to break the cycle of "build-sink-rebuild." Although the unit cost exceeds current Huntap caps, it eliminates the hidden costs of delay, difficult logistics, and inevitable foundation failure.
By adopting a tiered pricing strategy and recognizing the "Foundation-First" approach, the Indonesian government and international NGOs can deliver housing that is not just "instant," but permanently safe, dignified, and resilient to the volatile future of the archipelago.
