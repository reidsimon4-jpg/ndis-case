1. Executive opinion
1.1 Bottom line
In my assessment, Claude’s revised strategy has the right centre of gravity—legality first, Sunflower
front and centre, and separate protection for Vida—but it still treats several discretionary or contingent
propositions as if they were settled. The strategy should be recast as a disciplined sequence of
applications and fallbacks rather than a single knockout theory.
The Reids should not consent to the respondent’s August 2026 proposed minute in its present form.
That minute appears to require the applicants to commit their full merits evidence before the
Commission has provided a final applicant-specific allegation schedule, stated the exact order it now
asks the Tribunal to make, resolved the outstanding s 70 access issue, or disclosed whether it relies on
the April 2026 statutory amendments. It also fixes a two-day joint merits hearing before the witness,
interpreter, privilege and cross-examination requirements are known.
The better immediate position is joint on common law and procedure, but separate on facts: one
concise common legal skeleton; individual statements of facts, issues and contentions; separate
evidence, privilege positions, loss schedules and settlement authorities; and express protection against
evidence about one applicant being used against another without notice and a fair opportunity to
answer.

1.2 Ranked conclusions
    1. Legality should be determined first, or at least in a first stage, under ART Act ss 53 and 79. The
       precise questions are statutory construction, identification of the operative command, certainty,
       severability, and the source of any new order now sought.

    2. Sunflower is the strongest authority. Its reasoning is highly material to wording such as ‘directly
       or indirectly’, ‘in any capacity’, and open-ended prohibitions on support, funding, guidance or
       assistance. It does not guarantee a no-order outcome: the Commission may distinguish the

3.1 What the severance reasons objectively indicate
The severance refusal should not be psychologised. The safe inference is institutional, not personal: the
member is concerned about delay, overlap, participant burden, efficient use of evidence and
inconsistent findings. The reasons also show that the respondent’s concise synthesis assisted the
Tribunal and that the member expects remaining privilege and timetable issues to be managed by
directions. Those signals favour a short, neutral, highly structured proposal—not allegations of motive,
bias, ‘paper mode’, chambers relationships or a plan to suppress a hearing.
A correction request under s 114 is available only for an obvious textual error. It should be confined to
provable inconsistencies in names, dates, citations or the recorded position of a party. It is not a
mechanism to reargue severance or revise evaluative reasoning.


4. Statutory architecture and temporal law
4.1 The source of the banning-order power
At the time of the 2022 and 2023 decisions, s 73ZN(2) authorised an order against a person employed or
engaged by an NDIS provider, or a member of key personnel, prohibiting or restricting that person from
engaging in specified activities if a listed gateway was satisfied. The expression ‘specified activities’ is
central. It is not a free-standing power to exclude a person from any direct or indirect involvement in the
entire NDIS ecosystem.
The April 2026 amendments did not remove the ‘specified activities’ limit in s 73ZN(2). They inserted,
among other things, s 73ZN(2B)–(2C), which can reach specified activities by applicants for registration,
approved quality auditors and certain people providing enabling or advisory services. They also inserted
s 73ZNA, creating serious criminal and civil consequences for conduct that breaches a banning order.

 Terminology
 The administrative-order argument is ultra vires: the order may exceed the power Parliament conferred.
 The 2026 Act itself should not be called ‘ultra vires’ without an identified constitutional challenge. Its
 relevance is temporal—whether the ART may use the new power in a review of an older decision and, if
 so, on what conduct and with what procedural fairness.

5. Sunflower applied to the Reid orders
5.1 What Sunflower establishes
Sunflower Care Services Pty Ltd v Commissioner of the NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission (No 2)
[2025] FCA 1442 is the lead authority. At [126]–[128], the Court distinguished a power to prohibit a
person from engaging in specified activities from a prohibition on indirect engagement or involvement.
At [129]–[134], it emphasised that the order must identify the prohibited activities with sufficient clarity
to permit exact obedience; explanatory examples do not necessarily cure globally framed operative
words. At [139]–[141], the orders before the Court were set aside. At [185] and [190]–[191], the
judgment reinforces the required nexus between identified activities, risk and proportionate protective
purpose.
Those holdings give the Reids a serious construction case if their operative orders prohibit ‘any
involvement’ in NDIS services ‘in any capacity’, directly or indirectly, and extend through open-ended
examples such as funding, support, guidance or assistance. The operative command—not merely the
reasons or examples—must be reproduced verbatim in the submission and compared line by line with
Sunflower.

5.2 What Sunflower does not establish

6. Can the law reach court before factual merits?
6.1 ART preliminary stage—necessary but not itself a court ticket
Sections 53 and 79 allow the Tribunal to confine the first stage to construction, severability, statutory
source and privilege procedure. This is the best way to put legality front and centre. However, a
preliminary answer that does not itself dispose of the proceeding may have no independent legal force
and may not be appealable immediately under s 172. Little Monster Productions v Screen Australia
[2026] FCA 902 is a direct warning against treating an interlocutory answer as an automatic Federal
Court bridge.

6.2 Section 185—preferred binding route
Section 185 is the express statutory route by which the ART may refer a question of law arising in a
proceeding to the Federal Court, with the President’s agreement. While the reference is pending, the
Tribunal cannot decide the proceeding inconsistently with the Court’s opinion. This is the cleanest path
to a binding judicial answer before factual merits.

It is not a right. A broad, hypothetical, mixed fact-and-law or outcome-framed question is likely to be
refused. The question should be settled only after the Commission states whether it accepts Sunflower,
whether it relies on severability, the precise substituted order sought, and whether it invokes s
73ZN(2B). If the Commission accepts the relevant construction, a referral may no longer be necessary or
appropriate.

6.3 Section 122—systemic ART route
The applicants may invite the President to consider referring the extant applications to the Guidance
and Appeals Panel on the President’s own initiative. The argument is that the permissible form of
banning orders, the interaction between Sunflower and the 2026 amendments, and consistent
treatment across the Commission’s portfolio raise nationally significant administrative-law issues. The
request should address both national significance and the interests of justice, including the Reids’ long
delay and Vida’s position.
A GAP referral can produce an ART guidance decision to which other ART members must have regard,
but it is not binding Federal Court doctrine. It can also enlarge the case, delay finality and increase legal
expense. It is a serious alternative, not a cost-free lever.

6.4 Section 32 stay
The 2026 breach regime materially increases the value of a stay application. An application for review
does not itself stop a decision operating. Under s 32 the ART may stay or otherwise affect operation or
implementation where appropriate to secure the effectiveness of review, taking account of affected
persons’ interests.
For each live order, the evidence should address current residence, absence of NDIS work or intended
re-entry, practical and reputational effects, compliance uncertainty, the absence of participant contact,
and proposed safeguards. A narrow alternative could stay the disputed indirect/in-any-capacity limbs
while the applicants undertake, without admission, not to provide direct participant supports, control a
registered provider or access participant funds. The Tribunal may nevertheless refuse because
protective risk and the historical allegations remain contested.

6.5 Direct Federal Court proceedings
Judiciary Act s 39B jurisdiction remains legally available in an appropriate case, but direct judicial review
now carries serious discretionary risk. McGowan v Migration Agents Registration Authority [2003] FCA
482 is adverse where active merits review offers a convenient and satisfactory remedy and parallel
litigation would fragment the dispute. Sunflower distinguished McGowan on unusual facts: the Court
case was already advanced, the delay was short and the same factual controversy was already before
the Court.
The Reids’ long-running ART proceedings make the alternative-remedy objection stronger, not weaker.
Refusal of severance, preliminary determination, a GAP request or s 185 referral does not itself establish
jurisdictional error. Direct court filing should occur only after written advice from senior Australian
public-law counsel identifies a ripe jurisdictional error, explains why ART relief is practically inadequate,
and addresses delay, extension, costs, security for costs and the utility of relief for each applicant. ADJR
adds no clear advantage and has its own time and discretionary barriers.

7.3 Why two days is premature
The objection is practical. A joint matter involving multiple applicants, contested documents, potentially
serious dishonesty and intimidation allegations, maker cross-examination, an interpreter, s 70 issues and
privilege rulings may require more than two days. Conversely, a successful first-stage construction ruling
or agreement may materially reduce the factual case. The only responsible estimate is one made after
the issues and witnesses are identified. The applicants should provide provisional ranges and time
estimates for each targeted witness rather than an unbounded demand.


8. Evidence, fairness and the applicant-specific case
8.1 Receipt is not acceptance
Section 52 means the ART is not bound by formal rules of evidence. Police records, participant
statements, internal investigation documents and hearsay may be received. The applicants should not
promise that the Commission’s file will be excluded wholesale. The stronger submission is that receipt
does not establish truth or weight, particularly where serious allegations are untested, incomplete,
second-hand or inconsistent with an adjudicated outcome.
For fraud, dishonesty, intimidation and similar allegations, the ordinary civil standard remains. The
gravity and consequences of the proposed finding bear on the cogency required; inexact proof,
indefinite testimony and conjecture are insufficient. Re Day is a useful serious-allegation analogue. The
submission should avoid inventing a separate ‘Briginshaw standard’.

8.2 Maker attendance and Byers
The ART Common Procedures Practice Direction supplies the practical mechanism. Identify only material
makers and state the document, contested proposition, reason cross-examination is necessary and
estimated time. If the proponent does not make a material author available after a proper request or
direction, seek the graduated consequences in Practice Direction 6.4: exclusion, reduced weight or no
weight as appropriate. A blanket demand for every document author is unlikely to assist.
Byers and NDIA [2024] ARTA 183 should be used only as a fact-specific illustration that particular police
material may be excluded or given limited effect. It is not binding authority for automatic exclusion. A
dismissal, discontinuance or acquittal means there was no conviction in that process; it does not
affirmatively prove the alleged conduct did not occur on the civil administrative standard.
Jones v Dunkel is secondary. A modest inference may arise where existing evidence calls for an answer
and an available witness who could elucidate it is not called without explanation. It cannot fill a gap,
prove the opposite or reverse the merits-review task. Participant welfare or intimidation concerns may
also explain non-attendance. Practice Direction 6.4 is the more direct route.

9. Vida’s self-incrimination and penalty position
9.1 The safe legal position
ART Act s 293(3) gives a witness the protections of a High Court witness. The common-law privilege
against self-incrimination is fundamental absent clear statutory abrogation. The risk must be real and
appreciable, not tenuous, illusory or virtually without substance. The severance reasons’ citation to Reid
v Howard should be checked as [1995] HCA 40; (1995) 184 CLR 1.
The privilege is not a blanket right to file no merits case. It ordinarily operates question by question,
document by document or by a sufficiently defined category. Evidence Act s 128 does not automatically
confer a court certificate in ART proceedings. A private hearing or s 70 order provides confidentiality,
not use or derivative-use immunity. Section 117 also creates consequences for failing to answer a
question the Tribunal lawfully requires, so privilege must be raised and determined before an answer is
compelled.

9.3 Penalty privilege
A separate penalty-privilege submission should be preserved but not presented as settled. Rich v ASIC
supports examining substance and consequence rather than accepting a purely protective label. A long,
stigmatic banning order backed by criminal and civil consequences creates a respectable analogy, but
the application of penalty privilege to this statutory merits review appears unresolved. Counsel should
address purpose, duration, stigma, deterrence, statutory structure and any separate civil-penalty
exposure.


10. The s 70 / legal professional privilege dispute
10.1 Reframe the opposition
The existing opposition is too categorical on waiver and adverse inference. The correct starting point is
the statutory separation between production to the Tribunal and access by parties. Sections 23 and 26
govern relevant and additional documents; s 29 addresses lodgment while restriction is determined; s
30 addresses privilege and public interest; ss 70–71 govern disclosure restrictions and the balancing
exercise.
The applicants should seek confirmation that the two identified advice attachments—Attachment O to
EC22-002103 and Attachment G to EC22-002521—have been lodged unredacted with the Tribunal,
together with a document-level schedule stating author, recipient, date, dominant purpose,
confidentiality and asserted waiver circumstances. The Tribunal can inspect in camera and determine
whether non-privileged source records or factual annexures can be severed.

10.2 Waiver and fairness
Under Mann v Carnell, waiver turns on objective inconsistency between conduct and maintaining
confidentiality, informed by fairness. Merely stating that legal advice existed, referring to a file label or

general legal concern, attaching a source record to advice, or defending the administrative decision does
not necessarily waive the advice. Physical attachment is not the test. Expense Reduction Analysts
reinforces the importance of substance and context.
Facts embedded in a privileged communication may themselves remain protected as part of that
communication. Independently existing records do not become privileged merely because they were
sent to lawyers. The order sought should therefore be segmented: disclose independently existing and
severable non-privileged material; preserve genuine advice; and require a fair factual gist or non-
reliance direction so no undisclosed adverse fact is used against an applicant. VEAL supports disclosure
in substance of credible, relevant and significant adverse information, not disclosure of legal advice as
such.

 Do not overreach
 Do not call the advice a ‘smoking gun’, ask the Tribunal to infer that it was unfavourable, or assume
 selective disclosure waived the whole subject. The advice could show reasonable care and damage a later
 misfeasance case. Keep the merits case independently provable.



10.3 Use of produced material
Documents produced compulsorily in the ART are ordinarily subject to an implied undertaking and the
Common Procedures Practice Direction. They should not be used for CDDA, damages, media or public
campaigning without Tribunal permission or an independent lawful source. This restriction must be built
into the evidence-preservation protocol.


11. Relief and the effect of a favourable ART decision
11.1 Primary and fallback merits outcomes
The primary relief should be applicant-specific: set aside the internal-review decision under s 105(c)(i)
and substitute a decision that no banning order be made. That must be supported not only by Sunflower
form arguments but also by a merits case addressing current gateway, evidence, necessity, activity
nexus, duration and proportionality.
Fallback relief should be pleaded without concession. If the Tribunal considers some protection
necessary, require the Commission to propose the narrowest lawful order: precise activities; direct
nexus; objective boundaries; workable conditions; minimum justified duration; and an express review
mechanism. The applicants should not draft the Commission’s case for it, but a lawful alternative
protects against an unnecessarily broad substitute.

11.2 Section 108 timing
If the Tribunal substitutes a decision, s 108(4) ordinarily gives it effect from the time the reviewable
decision had effect. Here the reviewable decision is likely the 21 April 2023 internal-review decision, not
necessarily the original December 2022 decision. Therefore the default may not erase the original-order
period. If the legal objective is a no-order position from the first effective date, counsel should seek an
express s 108(5) order specifying that earlier date and explain why the statute authorises and justice
requires it. This must be tied to the exact review jurisdiction and instruments.

11.3 Consent resolution and reasons
An agreed ART disposition under s 103 can be valuable, but s 111’s mandatory written-reasons regime
excludes decisions made by agreement under s 103(2). The Reids should not assume entitlement to
Tribunal-authored ‘agreed reasons’. Any corrective statement, factual non-admission, register action,
publication or explanation must be negotiated as a separate binding term, subject to what the
Commission can lawfully promise.

11.4 Register consequences
Current s 73ZDA requires active-ban information and permits historical-ban information. A
retrospectively substituted no-order decision would strengthen a correction argument, but deletion is
not automatic. The current Provider Register Rules may fit providers more clearly than individuals who
were employees or key personnel. Counsel should consider: statutory correction or annotation; an APP
13 personal-information correction request; a statement associated with disputed information;
nonpublication where lawful; and negotiated undertakings. The target should be accuracy and legal
authority, not an assumption that history can be erased.


12. Compensation and other forums
12.1 The ART cannot award damages
A favourable ART outcome may improve later proof of wrongfulness, counterfactual and causation, but
it does not create a damages entitlement. Compensation work should run in parallel as preservation and
quantification, while substantive pursuit is sequenced to avoid waiver, duplication and limitation
prejudice.

12.2 CDDA and legal-liability settlement
The Scheme for Compensation for Detriment caused by Defective Administration is discretionary and
creates no legal entitlement. RMG 409 requires identified defective administration, direct causal
connection, quantifiable detriment and mitigation, and generally excludes losses for which the
Commonwealth is legally liable. Where there is a meaningful prospect of legal liability, the Legal Services
Directions settlement pathway and Attorney-General’s Department Guidance Note 14 are the more
appropriate classification.
The routes should be pleaded in the alternative, not as cumulative pressure for the same loss. An ART
result may be evidence, but does not establish defective administration, negligence, misfeasance,
causation or quantum. Separate Simon, Vida, FFG and other-entity schedules must identify who suffered
each loss, the counterfactual, documents, mitigation and any overlap with loss caused by the earlier
narrower order rather than the later variation.

12.3 Misfeasance
Misfeasance in public office is presently a preservation issue, not a pleaded conclusion. Sanders v Snell
shows that invalidity, administrative error, unfairness or foreseeable loss is insufficient. The case
requires identification of a natural-person public officer, an invalid public act, actual knowledge or
subjective reckless indifference to lack of power, the necessary targeted harm or
knowledge/recklessness as to likely harm, causation and loss. A later Sunflower ruling does not
retroactively prove an officer’s state of mind. Continued action after clear legal notice may be a separate
evidentiary episode, but only officer-specific documents can make the claim viable.
