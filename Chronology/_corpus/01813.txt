## Executive recommendation

The present working preference is to settle and commence a narrow s 39B Federal Court proceeding on the facial statutory-authority ground, subject to counsel and completion of the non-privileged chronology from 21 November 2025 to early March 2026.

That preference is based on the authenticated current ART history:

- the merits reviews commenced on 10 May 2023;
- severance was placed on a papers pathway on 19 March 2026;
- on 22 April the Registry said the severance decision “remains under consideration”;
- on 19 May the Registry said the Commission’s s 70 application had been put before the Deputy President for consideration;
- on 24 June Simon requested confirmation, status and expected delivery dates for both issues;
- that email received no reply; and
- by 15 July no severance outcome, s 70 outcome, direction or substantive listing had been communicated by email.

The ART communicates decisions, directions and listings in these matters by email. The absence of a communicated outcome is therefore evidence of the practical state of the alternative remedy, not merely a portal-search inference.

A s 185 reference and an unreasonable-delay application remain possible. Neither should be treated as an automatic preliminary step. A s 185 reference may itself delay the ART decision under s 185(5). A delay proceeding can compel a decision, not historical invalidity, and may simply produce a substituted ban.

## 1. Governing discretion

*Sunflower Care Services Pty Ltd v Commissioner of the NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission (No 2)* [2025] FCA 1442 at [117]–[120] must be addressed on both sides.

Under *Sunflower* and *Bechara v Bates* [2021] FCAFC 34; 286 FCR 166 at [157]–[164], a clear want or excess of jurisdiction affecting an aggrieved party ordinarily attracts constitutional relief almost as of right unless it is just to withhold it. That is the affirmative starting point.

The Court may nevertheless withhold relief because a more convenient and satisfactory remedy exists, delay is unreasonable, relief lacks utility, or no real injustice remains. *McGowan v Migration Agents Registration Authority* [2003] FCA 482; 129 FCR 118 is the adverse comparator: an extant AAT merits review was held to be more satisfactory.

The Reids should not argue that the ART is legally powerless. Sections 32, 54 and 105 of the *Administrative Review Tribunal Act 2024* (Cth) permit substantial prospective relief. The answer is that the actual review has not produced a final outcome or communicated substantive timetable after more than three years, while the orders continue to operate.

## 4. Why the facial ground remains the lead case

The strongest issue is documentary:

1. identify the exact words of each original and review banning order;
2. identify former s 73ZN(2) in force when each order was made;
3. apply *Sunflower* at [127]–[141]; and
4. ask whether the instrument prohibited specified activities or instead purported to regulate indirect relationships, statuses and forms of involvement beyond the former power.

That issue should not require Simon to describe alleged criminal conduct, operational arrangements or disputed business facts. It can proceed on the official instruments, reasons, statutory texts and authenticity evidence.

The procedural-fairness, omitted-record, independent-assessment and illogicality grounds remain separate. They are potentially important, particularly for Simon, but invite a larger evidence dispute. Counsel should seek determination of the facial ground first and hold fact-dependent grounds over if necessary.

## 5. Applicant-specific utility

### Vida

Vida appears to have the cleaner instrument case because her original order itself used broad “directly or indirectly” language. Relief directed to the original and review instruments may therefore have direct historical and prospective utility.

Her decision to cease pressing a stay application must be described candidly. It is not fatal to final relief, but it will be relevant to urgency and interim protection. If an insurer has declined, withdrawn or made cover unavailable by reference to the wording or published characterisation in either order, the written decision is useful current-harm evidence. It supports utility and later loss analysis, but should not be pleaded as a separate insurance dispute or as proof that the Commission caused every uninsured loss.

### Simon

Simon has the revival problem. If only the 2023 review order is quashed, the original 2022 order may remain or revive. The relief must identify the legal effect of review and challenge every layer needed for a useful outcome.

The omitted-record material may support procedural fairness and failure genuinely to assess Simon separately. His affidavit should remain confined. The Commission record should carry the case wherever possible.

### Both applicants

The current form of s 73ZN may permit a fresh order expressed in terms unavailable under former s 73ZN(2). Judicial review seeks correction of the historical instruments, not immunity from a lawful future decision. A possible new order limits finality but does not retrospectively validate years of operation under an instrument beyond the former power.

## 6. The unresolved post-Sunflower period

The March-to-July ART delay is well supported. The explanation for why the Reids did not rely on *Sunflower* earlier is now more precise: Simon first found it on 15 July 2026 while self-represented and searching AustLII for reported decisions and case references to use in the ART matters. The Reids had solicitors and counsel at earlier stages, but could not sustain further representation after the costs incurred over years of litigation. This is a candid explanation, not a complete answer: the affidavit must give the periods and limits of earlier representation, objective cost evidence if relied upon, and the prompt steps taken after discovery. No allegation should be made that any former lawyer was at fault or that legal advice should be disclosed.

The common affidavit must still establish:

- that Simon first learned of *Sunflower* on 15 July 2026 while researching AustLII for ART case references;
- what non-privileged objective steps were taken before early March 2026;
- what documents were unavailable and which separate ground required them;
- whether representation, health, finances or case programming affected timing; and
- why the facial ground was not filed earlier.

The May 2026 production explains Simon’s personal-record ground. It does not alone explain the facial-ground period from November 2025.

No filed document should disclose the substance of legal advice.

## 7. Possible unreasonable-delay relief against the ART

Section 7 of the *Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act 1977* (Cth) permits an aggrieved person to seek review where a person has a duty to make a decision to which the Act applies, no period is prescribed, the decision has not been made and the delay is unreasonable. Section 16(3) permits an order directing the making of the decision and a declaration of rights.

Counsel must determine:

- the precise decision and office-holder subject to a duty;
- whether the duty concerns the final s 105 review, severance or s 70;
- whether the interlocutory powers carry a legally enforceable duty to decide;
- the full allocation of earlier delay; and
- whether s 39B mandamus is cleaner than ADJR Act s 7.

The final review duty is textually stronger because s 105 says the Tribunal must make a decision. The interlocutory position is less straightforward.

The ART Act s 9 objective—resolution as quickly and inexpensively as proper consideration permits—is relevant statutory context, not a fixed deadline.

This route should remain separate unless counsel concludes that compelling an ART decision is more useful than directly challenging the Commission instruments.

## 8. Interim and case-management strategy

The absence of an ART timetable supports urgency but does not guarantee interim relief. Evidence must establish particular harm and balance of convenience.

The proposed Federal Court case should:

- ask for the facial ground to be determined first;
- rely on a tightly confined documentary record;
- preserve fact-dependent grounds if necessary;
- seek separate applicant-specific interim relief;
- preserve Simon’s ordinary unpaid family assistance to Vida and Alex if counsel considers that relief available; and
- avoid asking the Court to determine criminal allegations.

A final solicitor status notice may be sent if counsel considers it useful, but it must not create another indefinite waiting period. The unanswered 24 June request already establishes a reasonable attempt to obtain a timetable.

## 9. Relationship to compensation

A public-law win may assist later compensation strategy but does not establish malicious prosecution, misfeasance, negligence, malice or individual liability.

The banning-order case concerns Commission instruments. Any future case concerning the NDIA, Mr Halls or another individual requires separate proof of acts, state of mind, immunity, limitation, causation and loss.

Delay may increase the period and amount of loss, but it also raises mitigation questions about available stays and procedural choices. It does not suspend limitation periods. A written insurance refusal tied to the wording or published characterisation of an order may be important evidence of causation and loss, but coverage, exclusions, alternative causes and mitigation will require separate analysis. The compensation, preservation and standstill work remains separate and urgent.

No allegation of malice, corruption, conspiracy or reckless indifference should be added to the opening public-law affidavits merely to support a later damages narrative.

## Bottom line

The recent ART delay is now evidence, not assumption. It materially strengthens the discretion, urgency and utility case for immediate judicial review of the Commission instruments and weakens an open-ended s 185-first strategy. It does not replace the need to prove facial invalidity, complete the earlier delay chronology or settle Simon’s relief. A separate failure-to-decide case against the ART remains a reserve tool because it may accelerate merits review without resolving historical invalidity.
