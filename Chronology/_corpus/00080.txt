CONSTITUTIONAL ASSESSMENT — COORDINATED MULTI-AGENCY ACTION
Fraud Fusion Taskforce; NDIA · NDIS Commission · CDPP · AFP · ABF (Home Affairs) · ATO
Companion to Reid v NDIA — working memo to brief counsel — 14 June 2026
Status and caveat.  Working analysis to brief counsel; not legal advice and not for filing. It is deliberately even-handed: it identifies where the constitutional arguments have real traction and where they do not, because over-claiming here would be a credibility risk. Authorities are tagged [VERIFIED] or [PIN]. Nothing concedes any fraud or wrongdoing by any applicant; the allegations are contested in their entirety.
The question, and the honest short answer
Question.  Is the coordinated multi-agency action against the applicants — under the banner of the Fraud Fusion Taskforce, involving the NDIA, the NDIS Commission, the CDPP, the AFP, the ABF and the ATO — unconstitutional?
Short answer.  Not as a single, freestanding proposition — and it is important to be candid about that. There is no “Fraud Fusion Taskforce” statute to strike down; the Taskforce is an administrative coordination arrangement, and each agency exercises its own ordinary statutory powers. Inter-agency coordination is not, of itself, unconstitutional. The real constitutional traction is narrower and mostly defensive: it lies in Chapter III’s protection of a fair trial and against abuse of process, in the privilege against self-incrimination, and — combined with ordinary administrative law — in each agency having acted beyond power or for an improper/collateral purpose. Those are the arguments worth running; a blanket “the Taskforce is unconstitutional” claim is not.
1.  There is no “Fraud Fusion Taskforce law” to attack
The Fraud Fusion Taskforce is a Commonwealth operational/administrative construct (a multi-agency taskforce, co-led by the NDIA and Services Australia, launched November 2022). It is not a statute and confers no special coercive powers. Each participating agency acts under its own Act — the NDIA under the National Disability Insurance Scheme Act 2013 (Cth); the CDPP under the Director of Public Prosecutions Act 1983 (Cth); the AFP under the Crimes Act 1914 / Australian Federal Police Act 1979; the ABF under the Customs Act 1901 (and Migration/border powers); the ATO under the Taxation Administration Act 1953 [PIN]. The consequence: there is no single instrument whose validity can be challenged. The constitutional questions attach to (a) each agency’s exercise of its own powers, and (b) whether the aggregate subverted a constitutionally protected process.
2.  Coordination is not, of itself, unconstitutional — where it becomes unlawful
The Commonwealth executive has no general power to act or spend absent statutory (or other recognised) authority: Williams v Commonwealth (No 1) (2012) 248 CLR 156 [VERIFIED]. But coordinating the exercise of powers each agency already holds is lawful. Coordination crosses the line when (i) an agency acts outside its statutory power or for a purpose other than that for which the power was conferred (Padfield v Minister of Agriculture [1968] AC 997 [VERIFIED]); or (ii) the coordinated conduct subverts a constitutionally protected process — most relevantly, the criminal process protected by Chapter III.
3.  Where the real traction is
3.1  Abuse of process / fair trial (Chapter III) — the strongest line
Where coordinated agency conduct subverts the integrity of the criminal process, a court may permanently stay the prosecution. In Strickland (a pseudonym) v Commonwealth DPP (2018) 266 CLR 325 [VERIFIED], the High Court stayed Commonwealth prosecutions because compulsory ACC examinations — disseminated to the AFP and CDPP — contravened the privilege against self-incrimination and tainted the process. See also Moti v The Queen (2011) 245 CLR 456 [VERIFIED] (stay where the accused’s presence was procured unlawfully); Williams v Spautz (1992) 174 CLR 509 [VERIFIED] (proceedings for a collateral/improper purpose); Jago v District Court (NSW) (1989) 168 CLR 23 [VERIFIED] (the court’s power to prevent unfairness). Relevance: if a criminal matter is revived, evidence of coordinated coercion or collateral purpose feeds an abuse-of-process stay.
3.2  Privilege against self-incrimination / right to silence
Compulsory civil or regulatory examination powers cannot be used to erode the position of a person facing criminal jeopardy: X7 v Australian Crime Commission (2013) 248 CLR 92 [VERIFIED] (a charged person cannot be compulsorily examined about the charged offence); Lee v The Queen (2014) 253 CLR 455 [VERIFIED] (dissemination of compelled evidence to police/prosecutors caused a miscarriage of justice). Foundational: Sorby v Commonwealth (1983) 152 CLR 281; Reid v Howard (1995) 184 CLR 1; Lee v NSW Crime Commission (2013) 251 CLR 196 [PIN]. Relevance: if ATO (or any) compulsory powers were used against an applicant while a criminal matter was on foot, that is a discrete and serious vulnerability for the State.
3.3  No executive punishment without a court (Chapter III) — a ceiling, not a clear win
Only a court may adjudge and punish criminal guilt; the executive may not: Chu Kheng Lim v Minister for Immigration (1992) 176 CLR 1 [VERIFIED]. The difficulty is that Lim permits non-punitive executive action, and banning/exit measures are characterised as protective, not punitive (the Tribunal’s own framing in Abdullah [2026] ARTA 743 [VERIFIED]). So Lim is best used as a frame — that a constellation of agencies inflicted punitive consequences (business destruction, device seizure, tax penalties) before any conviction — rather than as a guaranteed invalidity argument. Run it candidly, as characterisation, not certainty.
3.4  Improper purpose / want of power — the realistic, winnable line
The most reliable arguments are administrative-law, not grand constitutional. Each agency must stay within its statute and its purpose. The NDIA had no power to ban/exit a provider (that is the Commission’s — see the companion application); ABF border-examination powers exist for border purposes, and using them “on behalf of another agency” for a domestic investigation is an improper purpose (the FOI records — “Law Enforcement interest”, “on behalf of another agency” — are the anchor); the ATO’s compulsion is for tax administration, not to pressure a litigant. These succeed on Padfield grounds without needing a constitutional ruling.

5.  Realistic forums, and the bottom line
Not a “strike down the Taskforce” suit. There is no statute to invalidate, and the underlying powers are valid. A freestanding constitutional challenge to the Fraud Fusion Taskforce would fail and would cost credibility.
The constitutional arguments are shields and levers within three realistic forums: (a) an abuse-of-process stay in any revived criminal proceeding (Chapter III; Strickland line); (b) judicial review and misfeasance of each agency’s conduct (improper purpose / want of power); and (c) oversight — NACC, the Commonwealth Ombudsman, IGIS (for any surveillance/interception), and the OAIC (the FOI reviews).
Lead with the verified and the administrative-law. The Padfield/improper-purpose and the NDIA-vs-Commission want-of-power arguments are the engine; the Chapter III and self-incrimination points are powerful but defensive and fact-contingent; the Lim “punishment without trial” framing is rhetorical support, not a standalone ground.
Preserve the record now: the self-incrimination point requires identifying any compulsory process used while criminal jeopardy existed; the abuse-of-process point requires the coordination/provenance evidence (the summons and FOI strategy in the matrix). Build before asserting.
