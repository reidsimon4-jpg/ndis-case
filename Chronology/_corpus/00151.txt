Part A — The central question, and the short answer
The question.  Could the NDIA lawfully do what it did to the Third Applicant — coordinate its exit from the sector, lock its payments and reassign its participants to a competitor — in September–October 2022, when no NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission finding, compliance notice, registration revocation or banning order yet existed? (The Commission’s banning orders were not made until 21 December 2022.)
Short answer: no.  By the time of the impugned conduct, the regulation of NDIS providers in Western Australia — registration, compliance, enforcement, market oversight and banning — was the exclusive statutory province of the Commission under Chapter 6A; the Commission assumed responsibility for WA providers on 1 December 2020 [PIN]. The NDIA’s functions (s 118) are scheme delivery, payments and financial sustainability, together with a payment-focused fraud-response function. The NDIA had no power to register, deregister, ban, declare a provider “unsuitable”, or engineer a provider’s exit — still less to do so without the procedural fairness the banning power requires. What the NDIA did was a de facto banning, achieved by an officer “leading a coordinated provider exit on behalf of the NDIA”, months before the Commission acted and without any of the findings, process or review rights the Act reserves to the Commission. That is beyond power and/or an improper-purpose circumvention of the Commission’s exclusive regime; and the Commission’s later orders cannot retrospectively authorise the NDIA’s earlier conduct.
Part B — The two regulators’ powers, weighed against the timeline
The Act splits provider regulation from scheme delivery between two different bodies. The conduct in issue fell on the Commission’s side of that line — and the NDIA acted first.
B.1  Which body holds which power
Reading: every lever the NDIA pulled against FFG — exit, reallocation, an effective “unsuitability” determination — sits in the left column (the Commission’s). The only lever in the NDIA’s column that it was entitled to pull was payment integrity on specific claims.
B.2  The timeline, mapped to whose power was being exercised
The weighing.  Place the two columns against the dates and the imbalance is stark. The NDIA’s only in-power lever was payment integrity on specific claims. Everything else it did — coordinating an exit, reassigning participants, treating FFG as “unsuitable” — was an exercise of functions the Act assigns exclusively to the Commission, and it occurred roughly three months before the Commission made any order. The NDIA achieved by executive action, and without submissions, the very outcome the Act says may be reached only by the Commission, only on defined grounds, and only after procedural fairness (s 73ZN). The Commission’s 21 December 2022 orders post-date the conduct and cannot cure it; and the Commission’s own reasons later tied the ban to an unresolved criminal process — confirming that, as at September–October 2022, there was no lawful provider-level determination of any kind on which the NDIA’s conduct could rest.
Part C — Jurisdiction invoked
Original jurisdiction under s 39B(1) and s 39B(1A)(c), Judiciary Act 1903 (Cth) [PIN] (mandamus, prohibition or injunction against an officer of the Commonwealth; matters arising under a law of the Parliament), and s 23, Federal Court of Australia Act 1976 (Cth). ADJR Act review is pleaded in the alternative only, contingent on the “decision under an enactment” question [PIN]; s 39B is led.
Part D — Relief sought
Declaration (want of power / usurpation). That the conduct of engineering the Third Applicant’s exit from the sector and reassigning its participants was an exercise of functions reserved to the Commission under Chapter 6A, which the NDIA had no power to exercise, and was therefore beyond power.
Declaration (improper purpose — payments). That the procuring of the freezing, locking or cessation of payments to the Third Applicant was for an improper purpose and/or beyond power.
Declaration (improper purpose — reallocation). That the reallocation of the Third Applicant’s participants to competing providers (including Diversitas WA Pty Ltd) was for an improper purpose and/or beyond power.
Declaration (procedural fairness). That the conduct achieved a banning/exit outcome without the opportunity to be heard that the Act requires before such a step, in denial of procedural fairness.
Certiorari / injunction / correction of records as available; and orders for preliminary discovery and production (Federal Court Rules 2011 (Cth), rr 7.22–7.23 [PIN]).
Mandamus (delay strand) directed to the Administrative Review Tribunal to determine the applications (including severance) and proceed to hearing within a fixed time; costs; and further relief.
Part E — Grounds
Want of power / usurpation of the Commission’s exclusive functions. The impugned conduct (provider exit, de facto deregistration, participant reassignment, an effective “unsuitability” determination) is regulatory action the Act reserves to the Commission (ss 181E, 73ZM, 73ZN); the NDIA’s functions (s 118) do not extend to it. Acting outside conferred power is jurisdictional error.
Improper purpose. The powers actually held by the NDIA (payments; participant management) were exercised for a purpose other than that for which they were conferred — to remove FFG and benefit competing providers — contrary to Padfield v Minister of Agriculture [1968] AC 997 [VERIFIED]; R v Toohey; Ex parte Northern Land Council (1981) 151 CLR 170 [VERIFIED]; Thompson v Randwick Corporation (1950) 81 CLR 87 [VERIFIED].
Procedural fairness / circumvention of statutory process. The conduct produced a banning/exit outcome without the opportunity to make submissions that s 73ZN requires before a banning order, denying the applicants the very process the Act guarantees. Using one body’s informal powers to achieve another body’s formal outcome, while bypassing that body’s safeguards, is an abuse of power.
Legal unreasonableness (alternative). Minister for Immigration v Li (2013) 249 CLR 332 [VERIFIED].
Constructive failure to exercise jurisdiction (delay strand). Plaintiff S297/2013 [2014] HCA 24; (2014) 255 CLR 179 [PIN]; Thornton v Repatriation Commission (1981) 52 FLR 285 [PIN].

NDIA’s likely defence | The answer
“s 118 authorised it — fraud response, financial sustainability, safeguarding.” | s 118(1)(ba) is confined to “claims for payment, the payment of NDIS amounts or the use of funding” — payment integrity, not provider banning. Banning, “unsuitability”, exit and market oversight are ss 181E / 73ZM / 73ZN — the Commission. The NDIA cannot relabel a regulatory act as payment integrity (Padfield).
“It was a routine payment review / compliance lock.” | A lock on specific dubious claims may be within power; a “coordinated provider exit” and the reassignment of all participants is de facto deregistration and exceeds any payment-integrity rationale.
“Participant safeguarding / choice (s 118(1)(a)(v)).” | Safeguarding is balanced against choice; here choice was overridden by direction and inducement (EM-030, EM-031). Managing provider withdrawal to protect participants is itself the Commission’s market-oversight function (s 181E(i)). Inducements (“shut up money”; a child’s plan) are not safeguarding.
“Information-sharing with the Commission was authorised (ss 66–67A).” | Sharing may be authorised; that does not authorise the underlying exit/reassignment, and authorised sharing of improperly-purposed material does not cleanse the taint.
“These were not reviewable decisions; the avenue is internal review + ART (ss 99–103).” | Precisely — the conduct was not a reviewable decision and gave no review, which is why s 39B and declarations are the only avenue, and why the conduct denied the protections the Commission’s regime builds in. Channelling presupposes a decision within the regime; there was none.
“Operational conduct — not justiciable / not under an enactment.” | s 39B reaches an officer’s conduct; want of power and improper purpose are justiciable; misfeasance reaches conduct regardless of whether it was a formal “decision”.
“Good-faith immunity.” | There is none for NDIA officers in the Act — ss 198–199 protect only nominees acting under s 74. No statutory shield; misfeasance is not barred.
“Delay / discretion / it is all spent 2022 conduct.” | Continuing conduct to 5 Jun 2026; declarations; misfeasance limitation preserved (Part G). The want of power is anterior and is not cured by the passage of time.
“The Commission’s later orders validate the concerns.” | Post hoc — they cannot retrospectively authorise the NDIA’s earlier conduct; and the Commission’s own reasons pegged the ban to an unresolved criminal process, not to any finding justifying the September–October acts.
