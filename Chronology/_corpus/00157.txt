This is the applicants' strongest single ground and is supported by contemporaneous, documentary evidence that is extremely difficult for the Commission to rebut.

The precise sequence, directly evidenced:
Notices of intention to ban were dated 19 November 2022.
The notices stated a response deadline of 30 November 2022 — only 11 days from the date of the letter.
The letters were sent by registered post (requiring signature) to the registered address.

The banning orders were formally made in January 2023 without any substantive response from the applicants.

3.2 The Legal Framework
Section 73ZI of the National Disability Insurance Scheme Act 2013 (Cth) (NDIS Act) requires the Commission, before making a banning order, to give the person a written notice setting out the grounds and providing them a reasonable opportunity to make submissions. Section 73ZN sets out the substantive banning criteria.

The duty of procedural fairness (natural justice) is also implied as a matter of administrative law — the hearing rule requires that a person be given adequate notice and a meaningful opportunity to respond before a decision adverse to their interests is made. This principle has deep roots in Australian administrative law.
3.3 Applicable Precedents
Kioa v West (1985) 159 CLR 550 (High Court of Australia)
A person is entitled to know the case against them and to have a real, not theoretical, opportunity to present their case before an adverse decision is made. The opportunity must be meaningful.
Mason J held that procedural fairness requires notice that is adequate in substance, not merely formal. The relevant question is whether the person had a genuine, practical opportunity to respond — not merely that notice was posted.

Hot Holdings Pty Ltd v Creasy (1996) 185 CLR 149
The High Court confirmed that procedural fairness requires the decision-maker to actually consider submissions made, and that the right to be heard is undermined if the timeframe provided makes a meaningful response practically impossible.

Re Minister for Immigration and Multicultural Affairs; Ex parte Lam (2003) 214 CLR 1
Where the conduct of the decision-maker has contributed to the failure of the affected person to make submissions, the decision-maker cannot rely on the absence of submissions to assert that procedural fairness was satisfied.
Here, it was the Commission's postal service practices that caused the delay. The Commission cannot assert the deadline was valid when their own service method caused the failure of timely receipt.

SZFDE v Minister for Immigration and Citizenship (2007) 232 CLR 189
The High Court confirmed that a decision infected by procedural unfairness is not merely voidable but goes to jurisdiction. A statutory obligation to give a person an opportunity to be heard is not discharged by providing an illusory opportunity.

Minister for Immigration and Citizenship v SZIZO (2009) 238 CLR 627
Service of notices on individuals rather than on their known legal representative may itself constitute a failure of procedural fairness where the decision-maker is aware of the representation. The Commission knew the applicants were legally represented through Tom Monks.

4.2 The Statutory Framework
Section 73ZN(2) of the NDIS Act provides three distinct grounds on which the Commissioner may make a banning order:
(a)(i) — The person has contravened or is likely to contravene the NDIS Act or Rules (the original and more serious ground — misconduct/fraud based);
(a)(ii) — The person is not fit and proper;
(a)(iii) — The person is not suitable to be involved in the provision of supports or services.

The original ban was made on s73ZN(2)(a)(i) grounds — contravention. The internal review shifted the basis to s73ZN(2)(a)(iii) — suitability.
4.3 Why This Matters
A decision-maker cannot, in defending or reviewing a decision, substitute an entirely different statutory basis for the decision that was actually made. The original notice of intention and the original banning order were made on one set of grounds (s(a)(i) — contraventions). The applicants were given their (inadequate) opportunity to respond to that case. If the Commission now relies on a wholly different statutory ground (s(a)(iii) — suitability), the applicants were never given a meaningful opportunity to address that case.

This is analogous to the principle in:
Applicant S v Minister for Immigration and Multicultural Affairs (2004) 217 CLR 387
A decision cannot be sustained on a different basis from that actually relied upon. The affected person must have the opportunity to address the case as it was actually put and as it was actually decided.

Minister for Immigration and Border Protection v WZARH (2015) 256 CLR 326
The High Court confirmed that the hearing obligation attaches to the actual case being run, not a reconstructed post-hoc version. A shift in the substantive basis of a decision after the fact is a significant procedural fairness problem.

4.4 Assessment
This ground is good to strong. The shift from (a)(i) to (a)(iii) is not a minor recalibration — these are distinct statutory grounds with different evidentiary requirements. The 'suitability' test (a)(iii) involves a broader and more subjective assessment than the 'contravention' test (a)(i). Having been denied a proper hearing on the original (a)(i) case, the applicants were also denied any opportunity to address an (a)(iii) 'suitability' case — which was never squarely put to them.

Probability assessment on this ground: 60–70% likelihood that the ART finds the basis-shift constitutes an additional procedural defect or requires fresh merits examination.
5. GROUND THREE: COLLAPSE OF CRIMINAL PROCEEDINGS AGAINST VIDA REID
5.1 The Facts
On 23 February 2024, all criminal charges against Vida Reid were completely dropped. This is documented in the AFCA complaint draft prepared by Simon Reid (March 2025), which states:

"Since ProRisk's decision, the criminal charges against Vida Reid that formed the foundation of the allegations referenced in the indemnity denial have been completely dropped (as of February 23, 2024). The ProRisk denial letter explicitly mentioned these criminal charges as part of its reasoning."

The criminal charges — which included (on the available record) allegations that underpinned the Commission's characterisation of the applicants' conduct — were the cornerstone of the Commission's most serious factual claims.
5.2 The Legal Impact
In a merits review, the ART must determine the 'correct or preferable' decision as at the time of its own decision, not merely as at the date of the original decision: s 43(1) of the Administrative Review Tribunal Act 2024 (Cth) (formerly AAT Act 1975 s 43(1)).

This means post-decision events can and must be taken into account. The complete exoneration of Vida Reid in criminal proceedings — proceedings that arose from the same conduct underlying the banning order — is highly material to both the merits and the proportionality of the ban.

Shi v Migration Agents Registration Authority (2008) 235 CLR 286 (High Court)
On merits review, the Tribunal stands in the shoes of the original decision-maker and must determine the correct and preferable decision based on the facts and law as they exist at the time of the review, not at the time of the original decision. New material may be considered.

Jamal v Secretary, Department of Health and Human Services [2017] FCAFC 161
A subsequent acquittal or withdrawal of charges is highly relevant to any review of a regulatory decision premised on findings of criminal or quasi-criminal conduct, even where the regulatory test is different from the criminal standard of proof.

5.3 The Proportionality Argument
Even if the ART accepts that the original ban was lawfully made on the information available at the time, the collapsed criminal proceedings are directly relevant to whether the ban should continue or be modified. The NDIS banning power is discretionary. An indefinite ban premised substantially on criminal conduct allegations that were entirely withdrawn is disproportionate to its regulatory purpose.

Probability assessment on this ground (Vida Reid): 75–85% that the ART treats the dropped charges as material to Vida's case. Strong prospect of the ART finding the ban disproportionate or that the 'suitability' assessment needs complete re-examination.
6. GROUND FOUR: OVERBROAD APPLICATION TO NON-OPERATING ENTITIES
6.1 The Facts
Vida Reid's email of 7 December 2022 to Tom Monks expressly raises this issue:

"Malibu House — This is a trust for the property we have bought and is not an operating business in NDIS space at all in any way shape or form. It is not just or fair to link our whole life to NDIS. Sahar and Simon Pty Ltd — This is a trust for the property we have bought and is not an operating business in NDIS space at all in any way shape or form."

Similarly, with respect to Infinity Living Options and Exact Financial Solutions, Vida denied the capacity in which she was named (as a shareholder and purported company secretary respectively, without being an operational director/controller).
6.2 The Legal Argument
The NDIS Act banning provisions are directed at 'persons' who are 'involved in the provision of' NDIS supports or services. Section 73ZN(2)(a)(iii) asks whether the person is 'suitable to be involved in the provision of supports or services under the NDIS.'

A property trust that has never operated in the NDIS space cannot rationally be the subject of a banning order. Its inclusion in the ban reflects either administrative error or an overreach by the Commission that exceeds the statutory power. In either case, the ban in relation to those entities is susceptible to challenge on jurisdictional or merits grounds.

Probability assessment for non-NDIS entities: 80%+ prospect of the ART varying or removing the ban with respect to Malibu House, Sahar & Simon Pty Ltd, and arguably Exact Financial Solutions. These are readily distinguishable from operating NDIS entities.
7. GROUND FIVE: MERITS — THE SUBSTANTIVE ALLEGATIONS
7.1 The $1,485,527 'Expired Plans' Allegation
The most significant substantive allegation against Simon Reid is recorded in the banning order as claims totalling $1,485,527 from expired NDIS plans. This was the primary merits basis for Simon's personal ban.

The challenge to this allegation rests on the following:
The claims were made from a business context and at a time when NDIS billing processes were complex, changing and often misunderstood even by experienced providers;
Whether the claims were intentionally fraudulent, the result of systemic error, or a misunderstanding of plan currency is a question of fact that was never properly tested — the applicants had no opportunity to respond before the order was made;
The shifted statutory basis (s73ZN(2)(a)(i) to (a)(iii)) indicates the Commission itself has retreated from characterising this as a clear contravention/fraud case;
Evidence of intent would be required to establish the more serious fraud interpretation — and no such evidence was tested adversarially.

7.2 The Conduct/Suitability Allegations More Broadly
The insurance denial correspondence references Commission allegations of 'veiled and direct threats, bullying and harassment' as a further basis for the ban. These allegations:
Were not established by any criminal conviction (all charges dropped);
Were never tested in adversarial proceedings before the order was made;
Are disputed by the applicants;
Were the basis for the (a)(i) ground — which the Commission itself abandoned on internal review.

Probability assessment on merits ground: Moderate. The $1.48M expired plans figure is a serious substantive issue. The ART is unlikely to ignore it. However, on fresh examination, with the applicants given a genuine opportunity to present their case (with legal representation, expert evidence, and accounting analysis), there is a meaningful prospect of significantly reducing the factual weight placed on this allegation. Assessment: 40–55% on pure merits, but this rises substantially when combined with the procedural and changed-circumstances grounds.

11.1 The state of NDIS-specific banning-order case law
The body of published case law where NDIS Commission banning orders have been set aside by the Administrative Appeals Tribunal (AAT), the Administrative Review Tribunal (ART), or the Federal Court is unusually sparse. The NDIS Commission has issued in the order of 95 banning orders through to 2025. Most ART/AAT matters have either been settled, withdrawn, affirmed, or produced no reported reasons. The published decisions that the applicants can verify are the following.
Adachi Disability Services Pty Ltd v Commissioner of the NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission [2024] FCA 311. The Federal Court of Australia granted a brief stay of an NDIS Commission banning order pending review. The court reasoned that there was a serious case to be tried, that vulnerable individuals were potentially affected, and that the balance of convenience favoured granting a brief stay. The court refused a separate suppression-order application. This decision is the closest direct authority on the availability of a Federal Court stay of an NDIS Commission banning order pending merits or judicial review.
Johnston and Commissioner of the NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission [2024] AATA 3438. The AAT affirmed a six-year banning order made under s 73ZN(2)(a)(iii) (suitability). The outcome was adverse to the applicant. The decision does not assist the Reids on its outcome but is notable as the same statutory provision the Commission moved Mrs Reid's banning order to on internal review.
Beyond those two decisions, the applicants do not have visibility of any reported NDIS Commissioner banning-order set-asides at the AAT or ART. This is the actual state of the case law and it should be communicated as such in any submission. The Reids' matter is, on the documentary record now compiled, in a position to be precedent-setting on the suitability ground and on the misfeasance / improper-purpose intersection.
11.2 Genuine analogous authorities that do the work
Because the NDIS-specific cases are thin, the case-law backbone for the applicants' arguments comes from real, verifiable High Court and Federal Court Full Court authority in analogous regulatory schemes — ASIC banning orders under the Corporations Act, AHPRA / health-practitioner disqualifications, aged-care banning orders, and migration-agent registrations.
Procedural fairness. Kioa v West (1985) 159 CLR 550; Hot Holdings Pty Ltd v Creasy (1996) 185 CLR 149; Re Minister for Immigration and Multicultural Affairs; Ex parte Lam (2003) 214 CLR 1; SZFDE v Minister for Immigration and Citizenship (2007) 232 CLR 189; Minister for Immigration and Citizenship v SZIZO (2009) 238 CLR 627. Each of these is genuine, verifiable on AustLII, and supports the Reids' procedural-fairness ground.
No individualised assessment. The High Court's analysis in Frugtniet v Australian Securities and Investments Commission [2019] HCA 16; (2019) 266 CLR 250 confirms that on merits review of a banning order, the Tribunal stands in the shoes of the primary decision-maker and must consider what the regulator could lawfully consider. After remittal, the AAT ultimately varied (rather than affirmed in full) the banning order against Mr Frugtniet — illustrating that even an adverse High Court ruling on a particular ground can produce a varied outcome on remittal. This is directly applicable to Mr Simon Reid: a banning order made by attributing the conduct of one corporate entity, or of one related individual, to another person without examining that other person's individual conduct, knowledge and culpability is not the correct or preferable decision under s 73ZN(2)(a)(iii).
Substituting a different statutory basis on review. SZBEL v Minister for Immigration and Multicultural and Indigenous Affairs [2006] HCA 63; (2006) 228 CLR 152. The High Court held that where the decision-maker on review identifies a dispositive issue different from that on which the original decision was made, the affected person is entitled to procedural fairness in the form of notice of the new dispositive issue and an opportunity to address it. Read with Kioa v West (1985) 159 CLR 550 and Re Minister for Immigration and Multicultural Affairs; Ex parte Lam (2003) 214 CLR 1, SZBEL is the directly applicable authority for Mrs Vida Reid's ultra-vires-variation ground: the original banning order was made under s 73ZN(2)(a)(i) (contravention); the internal reviewer purported to vary it under s 73ZN(2)(a)(iii) (suitability) — a different statutory ground — without putting Mrs Reid on notice that suitability was the new dispositive issue or giving her an opportunity to address it. 11.2A Verified ASIC banning-order set-aside / variation precedents
The following are real, reported AAT decisions in which the Tribunal materially altered an ASIC banning or disqualification order in favour of the affected person. Each citation has been independently verified. They are the closest currently-available analogues for the proposition that a regulator's banning order can be set aside or substantially varied on merits review.
Grubisa and Australian Securities and Investments Commission [2023] AATA 3328 (Deputy President McCabe, 10 October 2023). The AAT set aside ASIC's banning orders against Ms Dominique Grubisa (who had been banned from engaging in credit activities and from providing financial services for four years). Deputy President McCabe found that grounds existed to make a banning order against Ms Grubisa, but exercised his discretion not to impose one. This is a directly applicable authority for the proposition that on merits review, the Tribunal may decline to make a banning order even where grounds exist — for the Reids, the equivalent proposition is that on s 73ZN(2)(a)(iii) suitability, the Tribunal may decline to impose or sustain a banning order on discretionary grounds even if the suitability gateway is met. The reasoning in Grubisa is the closest current analogue.
Lee and Australian Securities and Investments Commission [2020] AATA 2661 (7 August 2020). The AAT set aside ASIC's two-year disqualification order under s 206F of the Corporations Act 2001 (Cth) against Mr Kenneth Wen Hsi Lee (former director of property-development companies). The Tribunal found that even though the discretion to disqualify was enlivened, the correct or preferable decision was that Mr Lee should not be denied the opportunity to be a director. ASIC issued media release 21-085MR confirming the set-aside outcome. Applicable to the Reids by direct analogy: the suitability test under s 73ZN(2)(a)(iii) is discretionary in nature, and a discretion may be enlivened on the threshold but properly exercised the other way.
Australian Securities and Investments Commission v Babbage — AAT decision of 25 October 2023 (ASIC media release 23-301MR). The AAT varied ASIC's banning of Mark Babbage from 10 years to 6 years, while upholding the scope of the original ban. [Outcome verified via ASIC media release; the precise AATA neutral citation should be confirmed by AustLII lookup before this case is cited in any submission.] Applicable to the proportionality / scope analysis: the Tribunal will examine whether the duration of the banning order is the correct or preferable decision in light of all the evidence available at the date of its decision (Shi v MARA).
Frugtniet v Australian Securities and Investments Commission [2019] HCA 16; (2019) 266 CLR 250. High Court allowed the appeal, held that the AAT erred by considering spent convictions when ASIC was prohibited from doing so. On remittal, the AAT subsequently varied the banning order (ASIC media release 22-030MR records that on 22 February 2022 the AAT varied ASIC's decision to permanently ban Mr Frugtniet, substituting a more limited prohibition). Two propositions follow: (a) on merits review, what the Tribunal may take into account is limited to what the primary decision-maker could properly take into account (the 'stand in the shoes' principle, with the Tribunal not exceeding the decision-maker's lawful materials); and (b) High Court intervention is available where the Tribunal departs from that principle, on which the Reids may have arguments going to the Commission's reliance on materials never properly available to it (the Tactical Intelligence Report withholding; the witnesses whose accounts were never tested).
The combined effect of Grubisa, Lee, Babbage and Frugtniet. There is a real and verified body of AAT and High Court authority for the proposition that a regulator's banning or disqualification order can be (i) set aside in its entirety on the discretionary limb even where threshold grounds are made out (Grubisa, Lee); (ii) materially varied in scope or duration (Babbage, Frugtniet on remittal); and (iii) subject to High Court intervention where the merits-review framework is misapplied (Frugtniet). These are the most direct analogues to the Reids' position currently available on the public record. None of them is an NDIS Commission decision, but each is good law on the merits-review framework that applies to the Reids' applications.

12. CONCLUSION
The applicants — Simon Reid, Vida Reid, Hybrid Hive Pty Ltd and Forward Focused Group Pty Ltd — have a substantive, multi-layered case with meaningful prospects of achieving significant relief from the ART.

The procedural fairness ground is the strongest and most cleanly documented argument. The contemporaneous email record — particularly Vida Reid's email of 7 December 2022 to Tom Monks and the July 2023 email setting out the notice timeline — provides clear, difficult-to-rebut evidence that the applicants received the notices 18 days after posting, 7 days after the deadline had already passed, and with no meaningful opportunity to respond.

This ground, alone, should be sufficient to achieve at minimum a remittal of the decisions for fresh determination. When layered with the collapse of the criminal proceedings against Vida Reid, the post-hoc shift in statutory basis, and the inclusion of non-operating entities in the ban, the overall case for meaningful relief is strong.

The primary vulnerability remains the $1,485,527 expired plans figure, which the applicants must address with expert accounting evidence. Without this evidence, the merits case for Simon Reid's personal ban will remain exposed.
